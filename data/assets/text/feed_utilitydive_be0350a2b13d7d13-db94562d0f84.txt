Nuclear is past the point of asking whether to use AI. Through ScottMadden's nuclear AI landscape research, we have identified more than 60 AI deployments across the U.S. fleet, spanning enterprise search, engineering support, corrective action, performance improvement, outage planning and equipment reliability. However, roughly 60% are in active use without benefits being effectively quantified. Users may report time savings or quality gains, but these have not yet been translated into labor savings, outage performance, risk reduction, or other measurable outcomes.
The more useful question for a nuclear executive, therefore, is not how many AI applications the organization has but whether those applications are changing how work gets done.
Three types of AI and why the difference matters
Three broad types account for much of what we see and requirements for data, integration, validation and governance rise as AI moves closer to plant decisions.
1. Generative AI for knowledge work. Document and procedure search, operating experience search, regulatory preparation and engineering assistants reduce the time skilled employees spend searching, compiling and drafting so they can spend more time applying judgment. Lower-risk uses with human review in place can often be deployed quickly.
2. Machine learning for repetitive decisions and prediction. In corrective action program (CAP) screening, AI can classify incoming condition reports, flag potential significance, recommend routing and elevate exceptions for human review. Outage schedule prediction is among the least mature applications, largely because historical schedule data is captured inconsistently across the fleet.
3. Specialized AI for engineering and operational optimization. Applications that combine AI with physics-based models or computer vision, such as core design optimization and equipment inspection, require the deepest integration with plant data and subject matter experts. Development is measured in years and talent combining machine learning with reactor physics is scarce. These applications also show the strongest evidence of sustained value: core optimization has been deployed fleetwide across multiple operating cycles.
There is more than one path to adoption
Utilities are taking three broad approaches and most run more than one. Enterprise platform rollouts, such as Copilot, scale fast but plateau without intentional change management and leadership reinforcement. Targeted vendor deployments bring nuclear-specific functionality but add procurement complexity, integration requirements and vendor dependency. Citizen development, where plant engineers build low-code tools within their functions, costs the least and delivers value quickly, but quality depends heavily on the skill of the team building it.
What separates scaling from stalling
In the programs gaining traction, the deciding factors are increasingly organizational rather than technical. One operator found that an AI-enabled observation program, built into a required process, was used consistently across its fleet, while an optional AI review tool for causal analyses saw uneven use and weaker gains. AI gains traction when a business owner redesigns the work around it and builds it into how that work is governed.
Five moves for nuclear leaders
1. Focus on the solution and desired outcome first, not only the AI tool. Narrow the portfolio to problems with meaningful operating value, an accountable owner, accessible data and a realistic path to implementation.
2. Challenge the process, don't just accelerate it. Before automating today's work, ask what AI makes possible: what can be eliminated, simplified, or reserved for human judgment.
3. Address integration early. Treat data access, system connections and the IT/OT boundary as part of the use case, not something to solve after the pilot.
4. Match governance to consequence. Set risk tiers, human review and validation requirements based on what an application does and what happens if it is wrong.
5. Measure what matters and scale what works. Tie priority applications to operating measures, such as engineering search time or outage planning cycle time, rather than licenses or usage counts.
From AI adoption to operating capability
Nuclear has demonstrated it can adopt AI. The next divide will be between organizations that accumulate AI tools and those that change how work is performed, measure the value created and govern the technology according to consequence. The utilities that make that transition will combine nuclear operating knowledge with trusted data, integrated processes and clear ownership of the work itself.
