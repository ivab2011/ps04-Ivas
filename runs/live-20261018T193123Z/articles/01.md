# The governance perimeter moved: from AI policies to operational obligations

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 1 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The important change for critical-infrastructure leaders is not that one new AI rule suddenly governs everything.  

It is that AI-enabled and agentic functions are being pulled into existing operational governance: access control, asset registration, third-party control, reliability modeling, communications, and public legitimacy. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c5]

That changes the executive question from “may we use AI?” to “who owns this function, who authorized it, what systems can it touch, what happens if it fails, and how do we explain the infrastructure burden?” [01-c5] [01-c3]

Industrial Cyber reported that FERC staff highlighted access-control risks as AI tools such as chatbots, personal assistants and automated analytical systems become embedded in networks supporting critical infrastructure protection assets. [01-c1]

That matters because these tools are no longer just productivity software at the edge of the enterprise; in the reported FERC framing, they sit close enough to Bulk Electric System cyber systems and associated information to require access governance. [01-c1] [01-c5]

The same reporting says FERC recommended that registered entities assess the business need for such tools, authorize and regularly review their access, monitor external communications, inventory them as generic service accounts, and assess vendor-related risks through supply-chain security programs. [01-c5]

Those are operational controls, not AI ethics principles. [01-c5]

A second Industrial Cyber item, discussing Ampyx Cyber’s examination of FERC’s 2026 CIP audit findings, reported that auditors found inverter-based generation resources operating above Bulk Electric System thresholds before registration or required cybersecurity evaluations. [01-c1]

It also reported cases where affiliates and third-party service providers monitored or controlled multiple generation resources from shared control centers that met medium-impact criteria, while individual operators had classified their systems as low impact. [01-c1]

The AI lesson is not that every chatbot is a grid emergency. [01-c1]

The lesson is that AI-enabled functions will be judged through the same perimeter questions that already apply to control centers, service providers and cyber assets: what is connected, who can act, and what classification follows from aggregated capability rather than local self-description. [01-c1] [01-c5]

The same perimeter expansion is visible on the other side of the meter. [01-c2] [01-c5]

Utility Dive reported that North American Electric Reliability Corp. stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centers, crypto mining facilities and other computational loads, moving them closer to final adoption. [01-c2]

The reported standards under NERC Project 2026-02 include requirements around interconnection studies and modeling, operational data and communications, load protection coordination, and disturbance monitoring. [01-c2]

That is a material governance shift: large computational loads are being treated not merely as customers negotiating interconnection, but as infrastructure whose behavior must be visible enough for planning, monitoring and reliable operation. [01-c2] [01-c5]

Utility Dive also reported that the standards are designed to give transmission operators, balancing authorities and reliability coordinators computational-load site data and communications protocols needed to prevent or mitigate reliability impacts. [01-c5]

For boards and executive teams, my interpretation is that AI governance now spans both sides of the meter: AI embedded in operational environments needs access authorization and monitoring, while AI data centers need reliability modeling and operational communications. [01-c5]

This is why a narrow “model risk” program is insufficient for critical infrastructure. [01-c5]

A model inventory may tell leaders what algorithms exist, but it will not by itself answer whether a shared control center changes impact classification, whether a vendor-enabled assistant has external communications, or whether a computational load can be coordinated during a disturbance. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c5]

The public-risk story is also moving into the governance perimeter. [01-c3]

On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun argued that local data-center backlash is often driven less by abstract fear of AI than by infrastructure burden, quality-of-life impacts, electricity use, distrust of corporations, and concern over deals arranged through opaque processes. [01-c3]

CSIS is offering reported field observations and interpretation, not a systematic public survey, so this should be treated as a source claim rather than settled measurement. [01-c3]

Still, the point is operationally relevant: siting legitimacy, energy burden and perceived bargaining power are becoming part of the risk environment for AI infrastructure. [01-c3] [01-c5]

Sun described residents objecting to nuisance effects, environmental and affordability impacts, and processes in which projects were negotiated under nondisclosure arrangements before communities knew what was coming. [01-c3]

She also discussed a Michigan data-center project expected to consume 1.4 gigawatts of electricity, which she framed as requiring substantial additional electrical infrastructure and potentially affecting emissions, construction and resident prices if companies did not cover resulting rate impacts. [01-c3] [01-c5]

The precise local outcome remains uncertain, but the governance implication is clear enough: public acceptance cannot be delegated entirely to permitting teams or utility interconnection staff. [01-c3] [01-c5]

Security practice is changing as well, but here the evidence needs careful labeling. [01-c4]

Dragos, an OT security vendor with a commercial interest in monitoring and response, says it is now seeing AI become field-relevant in OT defense, red teaming, incident response and early-stage adversary operations. [01-c4]

Dragos also says it has historically tried to reduce hype around claims that simple cyber activity could “take down the power grid,” but that current developments now concern its practitioners. [01-c4]

That is not independent incident statistics, and it should not be treated as proof that agentic AI has transformed OT threat models everywhere. [01-c4]

It is evidence that at least some OT practitioners now see AI as operationally relevant while still trying to distinguish practical risk from exaggeration. [01-c4]

The counterargument is that much of the strongest regulatory evidence here is US-centric, and some of it reaches us through trade reporting rather than the primary FERC report text.

EU regulator, operator, worker and affected-community voices are largely missing from this evidence set, which limits any transatlantic comparison.

The remaining uncertainty is not whether AI will be governed, but which existing obligations will attach first, how consistently auditors will interpret AI-enabled functions, and how much public legitimacy risk will be priced into infrastructure approvals. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c3] [01-c5]

For now, the safest executive move is to treat agentic AI governance as a perimeter-expansion problem. [01-c5]

Every AI-enabled operational function should have an accountable owner, an authorization path, an access and communications model, a vendor-risk treatment, and a failure or disturbance scenario tied to reliability obligations. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c5]

Every major AI infrastructure dependency should also have a public-risk story that explains energy use, infrastructure buildout, community process and who bears the costs. [01-c3] [01-c5]

## What to watch

- Whether final NERC computational-load standards preserve the reported requirements for modeling, operational data, communications, protection coordination and disturbance monitoring. [01-c2]

- Whether future FERC/NERC audit guidance treats embedded AI tools as service accounts, vendor dependencies, external-communications risks or some new control category. [01-c1] [01-c5]

- Whether data-center opposition continues to center on infrastructure burden and opaque process rather than AI as such. [01-c3]
