# What leaders should do next: assign ownership before autonomy expands

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 3 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

Critical-infrastructure leaders should not wait for agentic-AI-specific regulation to mature before assigning ownership for autonomous or AI-enabled functions in operational environments. [03-c1][03-c2][03-c3][03-c6]  

The immediate governance question is narrower and more practical: for every AI-enabled function, who can prove what it can reach, who authorized it, what infrastructure depends on it, and who outside the company is affected by it? [03-c1][03-c2][03-c3][03-c4]  

That is my interpretation of the evidence, not a settled regulatory formula. [03-c1][03-c2][03-c3][03-c6]  

The evidence points to four ownership gaps that boards and executives can act on now: AI-enabled access, third-party operational control, computational-load reliability, and data-center community impacts. [03-c1][03-c2][03-c3][03-c4]  

## 1. Assign an owner for every AI-enabled access path

Industrial Cyber reported that FERC identified risks from AI tools and third-party control systems in the US power sector, including the need for AI-facilitated access to be controlled, regularly reviewed and covered by existing risk-management frameworks. [03-c1]  

Ampyx Cyber’s reading of the FERC 2026 CIP audit findings turns that into an evidence test: for each AI tool, an entity should be able to show the CIP-004 authorization, the account it runs under, the BCSI it can reach and the communications it makes outside the ESP. [03-c1]  

That is a reported compliance-oriented expectation, not a speculative “AI safety” principle. [03-c1]  

For leaders, the management action is to require an inventory of AI tools in CIP-networked environments and map each one to an accountable business owner, a technical owner and an authorization record. [03-c1][03-c6]  

If the tool can communicate outside the protected environment, that communication should be treated as an access path requiring ownership, review and evidence. [03-c1]  

## 2. Bring shared control into governance scope before formal scope changes

Industrial Cyber reported FERC’s position that generator owners and operators remain responsible for demonstrating compliance for BES Cyber Systems that should be categorized as such, including systems operated by an affiliate or third party. [03-c2]  

That matters for agentic AI because autonomous or AI-assisted functions may be embedded in shared control arrangements rather than owned directly by the operator. [03-c2][03-c6]  

My inference is that operators should treat affiliate, vendor and contractor control as a governance-scope problem now, even before any formal registration change brings an aggregator directly into scope. [03-c2]  

Ampyx Cyber quoted a warning that, depending on how NERC responds, a registration change could bring the aggregator itself into scope. [03-c2]  

The uncertainty is whether and how NERC will draw that boundary, but the operational risk exists before the registration question is settled. [03-c2]  

Executives should therefore ask whether any third party can initiate, recommend or automate actions across multiple operational environments, and whether the operator can evidence authorization and monitoring for that activity. [03-c1][03-c2]  

## 3. Treat computational load as reliability governance, not only as growth

Utility Dive reported that proposed reliability standards for AI data centers and crypto miners are moving toward NERC Board consideration and, if adopted, FERC approval. [03-c3]  

Utility Dive also reported that the standards would apply to computational loads greater than or equal to 50 MW connected at voltage levels greater than or equal to 100 kV. [03-c3]  

That makes computational load a governance issue for executives responsible for AI data centers, grid connection strategy or large-load interconnection. [03-c3]  

The immediate action is to assign internal ownership for tracking whether planned or contracted computational loads fall within the proposed scope and what evidence would be needed if the standards are adopted. [03-c3]  

This is not only a data-center developer issue; it affects utilities and infrastructure operators that must plan around large, concentrated and potentially fast-growing loads. [03-c3]  

## 4. Treat community trust and cost allocation as governance evidence

The CSIS AI policy podcast reported local concerns that data-center projects can feel undemocratic when negotiations occur under nondisclosure arrangements and communities learn late what is being built, for whom and on what terms. [03-c4]  

The same CSIS discussion described concerns about whether small communities can negotiate fair benefits and whether they may face stranded-asset risks if a project fails or the AI market changes. [03-c4]  

Utility Dive separately quoted an AWS energy lead arguing that large energy users covering the cost of new infrastructure, combined with modern planning tools, can benefit everyone. [03-c4]  

That AWS view is a commercial source claim and should not be treated as neutral proof that cost allocation will be fair in every case. [03-c4]  

My interpretation is that data-center governance should include transparent engagement records, cost-allocation evidence and a clear account of who bears infrastructure risk. [03-c4]  

The limitation is that the community-engagement evidence here comes from US reporting and may not transfer cleanly to European permitting, grid-connection and public-consultation regimes. [03-c4]  

## 5. Make OT visibility the first control, but do not confuse a vendor claim with independent proof

Dragos says its passive monitoring approach can build asset inventories, learn normal communications and show device-to-device protocol activity without touching the process. [03-c5]  

Dragos also says passive collection has limits where assets rarely communicate, legacy equipment is quiet or segmented cells are dark. [03-c5]  

Because Dragos is a vendor, this is a source claim with a commercial incentive, not independent evidence that any specific product solves the governance problem. [03-c5]  

The governance point is still sound: teams cannot authorize, monitor or constrain AI-enabled tools against assets they have not discovered or baselined. [03-c5]  

For boards, the right question is not which monitoring platform is best, but whether management can prove the asset baseline against which AI-enabled access and behavior are being assessed. [03-c1][03-c5]  

## 6. Test vendor-AI change control in the next audit cycle

Ampyx Cyber says entities should know which products in their CIP-networked environments have AI features today, including features a vendor enabled after purchase. [03-c6]  

Ampyx also says FERC’s consideration under CIP-013 is to identify and assess risks from AI products or services from prospective vendors in the supply-chain risk-management plan, using a framework such as NIST AI RMF 1.0. [03-c6]  

This is a source claim about how audit expectations may be interpreted, but it gives leaders a practical control test. [03-c6]  

The next audit cycle should ask which operational products contain AI features, whether those features were present at procurement, whether they were enabled later, and whether supply-chain review covered that change. [03-c6]  

The hard case is where vendors obscure AI features or enable them through updates, because the operator may not have the information needed to evidence authorization and risk assessment. [03-c6]  

The remaining uncertainty is that regulator practice for agentic AI in OT is not yet settled, especially where autonomy is embedded in vendor products or shared operational-control arrangements. [03-c2][03-c6]  

The conservative response is to assign ownership before autonomy expands, rather than wait for regulators to define every category after deployment. [03-c1][03-c2][03-c3][03-c6]  

## What to watch

- NERC’s treatment of shared-control aggregators and whether registration changes bring aggregators directly into scope. [03-c2]  
- The NERC Board and FERC approval path for computational-load reliability standards, especially the 50 MW and 100 kV applicability threshold. [03-c3]  
- Audit expectations for AI features embedded by vendors, including features enabled after purchase and supply-chain review under CIP-013. [03-c6]
