# What leaders should do next: assign ownership before autonomy expands

[VISUAL: Title slide. “Before autonomy expands, assign ownership.” Source: Agentic GRC.]

Who, in your organization, can prove what each AI-enabled function can reach, who authorized it, what depends on it, and who outside the company is affected?

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Do not wait for agentic-AI-specific rules.” Source: FERC and Industrial Cyber.]

The practical message is simple.

Critical-infrastructure leaders should not wait for agentic-AI-specific regulation to mature.

The first job is ownership.

For artificial intelligence tools inside Critical Infrastructure Protection, or CIP, networked environments, Industrial Cyber reported that operators should be ready to show the authorization, the account the tool runs under, the Bulk Electric System Cyber System Information it can reach, and the communications it makes outside the Electronic Security Perimeter. [03-c1]

Industrial Cyber also reported that entities must control, regularly review, and govern AI-facilitated access under existing risk-management frameworks. [03-c1]

So the near-term question is not whether an AI agent is “fully autonomous.”

It is whether its access is approved, visible, reviewed, and owned.

That applies even when the AI feature is embedded inside a product the operator already bought.

Ampyx Cyber says entities should know which products in their CIP-networked environment have AI features today, including features a vendor enabled after purchase. [03-c6]

Ampyx also says supply-chain risk plans under CIP-013 should assess risks from AI products or services from prospective vendors, using a framework such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0. [03-c6]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Shared control is already a governance problem.” Key quote: “affiliate or third party.” Source: Industrial Cyber.]

The second job is shared control.

This is my interpretation of the evidence: operators should treat affiliate, vendor, and contractor control as in scope now, even before any formal registration change brings aggregators directly into scope. [03-c2]

Industrial Cyber reported that generator owners and operators remain responsible for demonstrating compliance for cyber systems that should be categorized as Bulk Electric System Cyber Systems, including those operated by an affiliate or third party. [03-c2]

Ampyx Cyber also warned that, depending on how the North American Electric Reliability Corporation responds, a registration change could bring an aggregator itself into scope. [03-c2]

That matters for agentic AI because autonomy often enters through service providers.

A vendor tool may recommend actions.

A contractor platform may coordinate operational changes.

An affiliate may aggregate control across assets.

If no one owns the control path, no one can prove the boundary.

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Computational load is becoming a reliability issue.” Key number: “50 megawatts at 100 kilovolts.” Source: Utility Dive.]

The third job is computational-load reliability.

Utility Dive reported that proposed reliability standards for AI data centers and crypto miners now go to the North American Electric Reliability Corporation board, and if adopted, to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission for final approval. [03-c3]

Utility Dive also reported that the standards would apply to computational loads greater than or equal to 50 megawatts, connected at voltage levels greater than or equal to 100 kilovolts. [03-c3]

So executives responsible for AI data centers, grid connection, or large-load customers should track this process closely. [03-c3]

This is no longer just a facilities question.

It is a reliability, planning, and governance question.

The same applies to community impact.

On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun described local opposition driven by opaque processes, nondisclosure agreements, concern about whether towns can negotiate fair benefits, and fear of stranded-asset risk. [03-c4]

Utility Dive separately quoted an AWS energy lead saying that when large energy users cover new infrastructure costs and utilities use modern planning tools, everyone benefits. [03-c4]

My interpretation is that data-center governance should include transparent engagement and cost-allocation evidence, because opposition may come from distrust of process and fear of ratepayer or stranded-asset exposure, not simply from opposition to AI. [03-c4]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Visibility before autonomy.” Key quote: “You can’t protect what you can’t see.” Source: Dragos.]

The fourth job is visibility.

This is a company claim from Dragos, a security vendor, not independent audit evidence.

Dragos says passive monitoring can build an asset inventory, learn what normal looks like, and detect threats without touching the process. [03-c5]

Dragos also says teams can inspect device communications and protocols, while noting that passive collection has limits when assets rarely talk, legacy equipment stays quiet, or segmented cells go dark. [03-c5]

The governance implication is conservative but important.

Operational-technology visibility remains a prerequisite for governing agentic AI, because teams cannot authorize, monitor, or constrain tools against assets they have not discovered or baselined. [03-c5]

[VISUAL: Closing slide. “What to watch next.” Three lines: “NERC aggregators. FERC load standards. Vendor AI audits.”]

What should leaders watch next?

First, how the North American Electric Reliability Corporation treats shared-control aggregators. [03-c2]

Second, whether the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission approves computational-load standards, and how the 50-megawatt and 100-kilovolt thresholds are applied. [03-c3]

Third, what auditors expect when vendors add AI features inside products already deployed in critical environments. [03-c6]

The evidence is still mostly United States-focused, and regulator practice for agentic AI in operational technology is not settled.

But the management agenda is clear enough.

Assign ownership before autonomy expands.
