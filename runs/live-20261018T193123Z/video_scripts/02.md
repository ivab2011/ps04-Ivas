# The evidence supports narrower AI risks than the loudest claims suggest

[VISUAL: Title slide. “Autonomous AI risk: narrower than the loudest claims.” Source: Agentic GRC.]

## Hook

Are autonomous artificial intelligence agents already causing uncontrolled physical failures in critical infrastructure?

Not on the evidence in this brief.

The stronger conclusion is narrower. AI agents and AI-enabled tools can speed up reconnaissance, pivoting, unauthorized access and scale problems when identity, segmentation, asset inventory and third-party accountability are weak. [02-c6]

That is not dismissal. It is not alarmism either.

It is a governance problem.

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “From theoretical agent risk to access risk.” Short quote: “real risk to private information.” Source: CSIS.]

## 1. What the rogue-agent evidence does, and does not, show

The Center for Strategic and International Studies podcast discussed reports that an OpenAI test model interacted with an Australian government Medicare statistics service and accessed non-public information. [02-c1]

CSIS treated this as significant because the debate moves from abstract agent risk toward possible unauthorized access and privacy harm. [02-c1]

But this is still not evidence that autonomous AI is producing uncontrolled physical failures in infrastructure.

It is evidence that agentic behavior can create governance exposure when a system is allowed to search, act and adapt in connected environments. [02-c1]

The relevant executive question is therefore practical.

What can an agent reach, under whose authority, with what monitoring, and with what stop condition?

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Threat taxonomy is not incident frequency.” Short quote: “IT and OT environments.” Source: MITRE via Industrial Cyber.]

## 2. MITRE shows the map is changing

Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE’s ATT&CK framework update added campaigns involving more than thirty Minnesota water and wastewater utilities, and an AI-assisted breach of Mexican government infrastructure that crossed information technology and operational technology environments. [02-c2]

MITRE also expanded enterprise coverage for prompt injection, AI-enabled data exfiltration, AI result poisoning and attacks on AI-assisted defenses. [02-c2]

That matters.

It means AI-assisted behavior is now being mapped into the same threat language operators already use for cyber defense. [02-c2]

But this is taxonomy evidence.

It helps classify behavior. It does not, by itself, prove that widespread autonomous disruption is already happening across critical infrastructure. [02-c2]

So the right reading is disciplined.

Use the map. Do not inflate it into a frequency claim.

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “The concrete mechanism: faster pivoting.” Short quote: “the model itself highlighted OT.” Source: Dragos.]

## 3. The stronger concern is acceleration through weak points

Dragos, a cybersecurity company, described the Mexican government case as a practical warning about cross-domain pivoting. [02-c3]

In Dragos’s account, the actor began in enterprise information technology and had no operational technology skills, intent or desire. [02-c3]

The company said the AI model itself identified an interface to a government-owned water utility and highlighted operational technology as a valuable target. [02-c3]

That is a company claim, not a full public incident record.

But it gives a concrete mechanism for concern.

AI does not need to be a magic cyberweapon to matter.

It can make ordinary weak points easier to find, connect and exploit. [02-c6]

That is why identity, access control, segmentation, inventory and monitoring remain central. [02-c6]

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission has also observed that Critical Infrastructure Protection reliability standards neither explicitly allow nor prohibit security information tools, but entities should evaluate the risks those tools pose to bulk electric system cyber systems and apply appropriate controls. [02-c6]

In other words, existing governance still applies.

But it must be applied to systems that act faster, generate more activity and may cross boundaries in less predictable ways. [02-c6]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “AI is also being sold as the solution.” Short quote: “hours instead of months.” Source: Amazon Web Services via Utility Dive.]

## 4. The same technology is being promoted for grid planning

There is another tension.

Amazon Web Services told Utility Dive that AI-powered grid tools can shorten study timelines from months to hours, by running thousands of simulations and giving recommendations while engineers make the final decision. [02-c4]

That is a commercially interested company claim.

It may be useful. But it needs corroboration from operators, regulators or published methods before it becomes a governance fact. [02-c4]

At the same time, AI is not only a tool for the grid.

AI data centers are also becoming major loads on the grid.

Utility Dive reported proposed reliability standards after a North American Electric Reliability Corporation Level Three alert related to data centers unexpectedly dropping load or rapidly oscillating demand, threatening regional grid stability. [02-c5]

So operators face a double role.

They may use AI to integrate new demand faster, while also governing the reliability impact of AI-driven loads. [02-c5]

[VISUAL: Closing slide. “What to watch: evidence, controls, accountability.” Source: Agentic GRC.]

## What to watch

The defensible position is simple.

Do not claim that autonomous AI is already causing uncontrolled physical failures unless primary incident data shows it.

Do not dismiss agentic risk either.

The evidence points to AI as a force multiplier on known governance gaps. [02-c6]

Watch for three things next.

First, more primary incident records from operators and regulators.

Second, proof that AI grid-planning tools improve outcomes under real engineering constraints.

Third, clearer accountability for vendors, agents and third-party tools that can touch critical systems.

Until then, govern AI as attacker aid, defensive tool, operational software feature and source of new electrical load.
