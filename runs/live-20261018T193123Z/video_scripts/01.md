# The governance perimeter moved: from AI policies to operational obligations

[VISUAL: Title slide. “From AI policies to operational obligations.” Source: Agentic GRC.]

One question: when an artificial intelligence system can plan, recommend, or act inside critical infrastructure, is it still only an AI policy issue?

The answer emerging from recent evidence is no. The governance problem is moving into operations. My interpretation is that leaders now need to treat AI governance as a perimeter-expansion problem, not only as a model-risk problem. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “AI tools inside access control.” Short quote: “authorize and regularly review access.” Source: Industrial Cyber.]

First, the access-control perimeter is changing.

Industrial Cyber reported that staff at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, or FERC, highlighted access-control risks as artificial intelligence tools, including chatbots, personal assistants, and automated analytical systems, become embedded in networks supporting Critical Infrastructure Protection assets. [01-c1]

That matters because Critical Infrastructure Protection rules are not abstract AI ethics rules. They govern access, authorization, monitoring, and accountability in the United States bulk-power system. [01-c1]

Industrial Cyber also reported audit findings on inverter-based generation resources operating above Bulk Electric System thresholds before registration or required cybersecurity evaluations. [01-c1]

The same reporting described affiliates and third-party service providers monitoring or controlling multiple generation resources from shared control centers that met medium-impact criteria, while individual operators had classified their own systems as low impact. [01-c1]

So the issue is not just whether an AI tool is accurate. It is whether the tool, the vendor, the account, and the control path are inside the right governance boundary. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Computational loads become reliability objects.” Short quote: “interconnection studies and modeling.” Source: Utility Dive.]

Second, AI infrastructure is moving into reliability governance.

Utility Dive reported that North American Electric Reliability Corporation stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centers, crypto-mining facilities, and other computational loads. [01-c2]

Those standards were developed under NERC Project 2026-02 and include requirements for interconnection studies and modeling, operational data and communications, load protection coordination, and disturbance monitoring. [01-c2]

That is a practical shift. AI data centers are not being treated only as commercial customers negotiating grid connections. They are moving toward explicit reliability obligations. [01-c2]

Utility Dive reported that the standards are designed to give transmission operators, balancing authorities, and reliability coordinators the computational-load site data needed to plan, monitor, and assess reliable operation of the bulk power system. [01-c5]

They also aim to establish communication capabilities with predefined protocols, so operators can act quickly to prevent or mitigate events that affect reliability. [01-c5]

My interpretation is that AI governance now spans both sides of the meter: AI embedded in operational tools must be authorized and monitored, while AI data centers must be modeled as reliability-significant infrastructure. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Public legitimacy is part of the risk model.” Short quote: “AI rarely came up.” Source: CSIS AI Policy Podcast.]

Third, the politics are more local than the AI debate suggests.

On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun said that, in conversations about data centers, AI itself rarely came up. [01-c3]

Her reported observation was that people were more often concerned about infrastructure burdens, quality-of-life effects, distrust of corporations, and deals that felt opaque or unfair. [01-c3]

The same CSIS discussion described concerns about traffic, lights, water, electricity use, emissions, new infrastructure, and possible electricity price impacts for residents. [01-c3]

This is a source claim from a podcast discussion, not a systematic public survey. But it points to a governance issue that executives should not ignore. [01-c3]

If communities believe infrastructure decisions are made under non-disclosure agreements or backroom processes, the risk is not only reputational. It can affect siting, permitting, timelines, and the legitimacy of critical infrastructure expansion. [01-c3]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Real concern, not panic.” Short quote: “What’s real, what’s hype.” Source: Dragos.]

Fourth, operational technology practitioners are taking AI risk seriously, while still separating evidence from hype.

Dragos, an operational technology security vendor, published a discussion called “Frontier AI in OT Defense: What’s Real, What’s Hype.” Dragos claimed it is seeing AI used in early stages of some incidents it has been involved in. [01-c4]

Because Dragos is a vendor, that should be treated as a company claim, with a commercial interest in the topic. [01-c4]

Still, the claim is relevant because it comes from practitioners describing field experience, and because they explicitly distinguish concern from exaggerated claims about infrastructure failure. [01-c4]

The useful takeaway is not that AI will suddenly take down a grid. It is that defenders now have to govern AI as a field-relevant operational risk. [01-c4]

[VISUAL: Closing slide. “What to watch.” Three phrases: “ownership, authorization, reliability story.” Sources: FERC, NERC, CSIS, Dragos.]

What to watch.

For executives, the question is no longer simply, “Do we allow AI?” It is, “Who owns each AI-enabled function, who authorized it, what systems can it reach, how is it monitored, and what happens when it fails?” [01-c5]

For data-center and utility leaders, the question is also, “Can this load be modeled, communicated with, and managed as part of system reliability?” [01-c2]

And for boards, the question is, “Can we explain the public-risk story before communities, regulators, or auditors force the issue?” [01-c3]

The limitation is that the strongest regulatory evidence here is United States focused, with limited European regulator and operator voices. So the transatlantic picture is still incomplete.
