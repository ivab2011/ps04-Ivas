# What operators should do now: govern agents as accounts, vendors, loads, and dependencies

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 3 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

Critical-infrastructure operators should not wait for a finished “agentic AI” rulebook before tightening controls around autonomous or semi-autonomous systems. [03-c1] [03-c3]  
The practical move is to treat agents through governance categories operators already know: accounts, vendor-enabled functions, external communications, computational loads, physical dependencies, and project legitimacy. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4] [03-c6]

For bulk-power entities, the clearest near-term implication is access governance. [03-c1]  
Industrial Cyber reported that FERC flagged risks from AI tools and third-party control systems, including the need for system integrator-facilitated access to be controlled, regularly reviewed, and covered by existing risk-management frameworks. [03-c1]  
Ampyx Cyber, interpreting FERC CIP audit lessons, says entities should be able to show, for each AI tool, the CIP-004 authorization, the account it runs under, the Bulk Electric System Cyber System Information it can reach, and the communications it makes outside the Electronic Security Perimeter. [03-c1]  
That is not a new agentic-AI framework; it is the translation of AI into evidence an auditor can inspect. [03-c1]

My interpretation is that operators should govern agents first as privileged or semi-privileged accounts. [03-c1]  
That means the inventory should not stop at “we use AI in this workflow,” but should identify which identity the tool uses, what it can query, what it can change, what sensitive information it can reach, and where it sends data. [03-c1]  
If an AI feature can call tools, retrieve operational context, or communicate outside a protected environment, the relevant question is no longer whether it is “just software,” but whether its access path is authorized, reviewed, and monitored. [03-c1] [03-c3]

Procurement and change management need the same treatment. [03-c3]  
Ampyx says documentation should include vendor questions about AI features, including features that can be switched on after purchase. [03-c3]  
That matters because the risk profile of a product can change after deployment if an AI function is enabled later by configuration, update, licensing, or vendor action. [03-c3]  
My interpretation is that security review should therefore include a “latent AI” question: what AI or agentic functions exist now, what can be enabled later, who can enable them, and what new access they would require. [03-c3]

IBM’s agent-engineering guidance points to a related control: constrain the agent’s tools and context. [03-c3]  
In IBM’s explanation, context confusion is a curation problem, and the agent does not need a large set of tools when only a smaller set is required for the task. [03-c3]  
IBM also says superseded information should be pruned, or the agent should be moved into a fresh context that restates the corrected state and leaves poisoned material behind. [03-c3]  
For critical infrastructure, the governance implication is simple: tool access and context windows are control surfaces, not implementation details. [03-c3]

Operators should also test for third-party control aggregation. [03-c1]  
If a vendor, system integrator, managed-service provider, or AI-enabled platform can touch multiple operational environments, then the relevant risk is not only one connection but the concentration of access across sites or functions. [03-c1]  
The evidence here is not a regulator’s full legal analysis of agentic AI; it is a combination of reported FERC concerns and practitioner interpretation of CIP audit lessons. [03-c1]  
That limitation matters, but it does not weaken the operational point: map the accounts, review the access, and keep evidence of the authorization basis. [03-c1]

The next governance frontier is load. [03-c2]  
Utility Dive reported that NERC Project 2026-02 includes proposed computational-load standards with requirements around interconnection studies and modeling, operational data and communications, and load protection coordination and disturbance monitoring. [03-c2]  
Utility Dive also reported that the standards would apply to computational loads of at least 50 MW connected at voltage levels of at least 100 kV. [03-c2]  
For operators and large-load customers, the watch item is not only whether a site uses AI, but whether its electricity demand creates reliability obligations. [03-c2]

My interpretation is that AI data centers should be governed as dependencies as well as customers. [03-c2] [03-c4]  
If proposed standards create planning, communications, and disturbance-monitoring duties, then grid operators, data-center developers, and large-load customers will need governance processes that join engineering, compliance, commercial, and public-affairs teams. [03-c2] [03-c6]  
That is especially important because public resistance may not be primarily about AI ideology. [03-c6]  
In a CSIS AI Policy Podcast discussion, Jasmine Sun said that in her conversations about data centers, AI rarely came up, while concerns centered on infrastructure effects, quality-of-life impacts, distrust of corporations, and backroom deals. [03-c6]  
The same CSIS discussion described concerns about new electrical infrastructure, possible rate impacts if companies do not cover costs, and opaque local approval processes. [03-c6]  
My interpretation is that transparency about electricity impacts, cost allocation, and community benefits should be treated as a risk control. [03-c6]

Physical resilience also belongs in the governance file. [03-c4]  
The Atlantic Council argued, after reporting disruption to Yandex and partner services from Ukrainian strikes, that data centers should be treated as critical infrastructure and given physical protections comparable to other strategic sites. [03-c4]  
It also argued that backups and dispersal should become routine features of digital policy. [03-c4]  
This is not direct evidence about AI-agent failure, but it is evidence that the infrastructure supporting AI services can become a critical dependency with physical exposure. [03-c4]  
My interpretation is that boards should ask whether backup, dispersal, and site-protection decisions are visible in risk governance, not left only to facilities engineering. [03-c4]

One useful analogy comes from outside AI. [03-c5]  
Industrial Cyber reported that Singapore’s Cyber Security Agency issued quantum-safe migration guidance recommending that exposed organizations establish migration plans by 2027 and complete transition by 2035, with Critical Information Infrastructure systems targeted for completion by 2031. [03-c5]  
The same report said pilot participants must report findings and demonstrated impact. [03-c5]  
This is not AI regulation, and it should not be cited as proof of what AI regulators will require. [03-c5]  
As an analogy only, it shows a regulatory method that could appear in AI governance: exposure assessment, named plans, deadlines, priority treatment for critical infrastructure, pilots, and evidence of readiness. [03-c5]

The counterargument is that current evidence remains fragmented.  
Ampyx is practitioner and vendor interpretation, not the regulator’s own text in this evidence set.  
Singapore’s quantum-safe program is a precedent for regulatory method, not an AI rule.  
EU-specific obligations, worker impacts, affected-community voices, and operators’ own implementation experience remain underrepresented.  
The remaining uncertainty is not whether operators need controls, but how far regulators will formalize them and how quickly those controls will become auditable requirements. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c5]

## What to watch

- NERC Board and FERC action on computational-load standards, especially planning, data, communications, and disturbance-monitoring duties for large sites. [03-c2]  
- Vendor updates that enable AI functions after purchase, especially where they add tool use, external communications, or access to sensitive operational context. [03-c1] [03-c3]  
- Whether regulators copy migration-style playbooks from other emerging-technology risks: inventories, owners, deadlines, pilots, and evidence of readiness. [03-c5]
