# The credible risk is AI-mediated access and acceleration, not magic autonomy

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 2 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The defensible risk claim is narrower than the loudest agentic-AI rhetoric: AI is not publicly shown to be independently taking over critical infrastructure, but it can change the path, speed and quality of access to operational environments. [02-c1]

That distinction matters for governance because controls built for “AI autonomy” in the abstract may miss the more immediate pathways: tool access, cross-domain discovery, poisoned context, unreliable recommendations and opaque dependencies in operational workflows. [02-c1][02-c2][02-c3][02-c4]

The strongest public example in this evidence set is not a fully autonomous attack on OT, but an AI-assisted breach that crossed from IT into OT context and reached a municipal water utility. [02-c1]

Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE ATT&CK v20 added campaigns including an AI-assisted breach of Mexican government infrastructure that crossed IT and OT environments and reached a municipal water utility. [02-c1]

Dragos gives a more operational version of the same case, saying it was called into an incident where an adversary was focused on enterprise IT, had no OT skills, no OT knowledge and no stated OT objective. [02-c1]

Dragos says the AI model identified that a system was an interface to a government-owned water utility’s OT networks and highlighted OT as a valuable target. [02-c1]

My interpretation is that this is the credible “agentic” risk pattern to govern first: AI can mediate access by surfacing operational context, recommending pivots or expanding what a non-OT attacker can notice. [02-c1]

That is materially different from claiming that autonomous agents are already controlling infrastructure on their own. [02-c1]

MITRE’s update also suggests that AI-specific threats have become concrete enough to map into known techniques and detections rather than leaving them as board-level speculation. [02-c2]

Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE expanded enterprise coverage with techniques addressing prompt injection, AI-enabled data exfiltration, AI result poisoning and attacks targeting AI-assisted defenses. [02-c2]

The same report says the release includes 15 new detection strategies and 38 analytics across enterprise and industrial-control-system environments, with industrial analytics designed to filter routine engineering and maintenance activity. [02-c2]

The governance implication is practical: operators should ask whether their AI risk register maps to observable behaviors, not only to broad scenarios such as “loss of control.” [02-c2]

If an AI claim cannot be tied to prompt manipulation, data movement, poisoned outputs, tool misuse, degraded defensive analytics or another concrete pathway, it should be treated as unproven until better evidence appears. [02-c2]

IBM’s agent-failure taxonomy is useful here, but it should be read as a vendor interpretation rather than independent incident evidence. [02-c3]

IBM argues that large context windows do not eliminate the problem of limited attention, because the model still has a finite attention budget even when the window can hold far more material. [02-c3]

IBM describes failure modes including context poisoning, distraction, confusion through tool sprawl and context clash when the window contains conflicting information. [02-c3]

IBM also points to a benchmark example in which a model failed when offered 46 tools but passed the same task when offered 19 tools. [02-c3]

The lesson for critical-infrastructure governance is not “never use agents,” but “validate context, compact history, curate tools and prune conflicting material before the agent is allowed near consequential workflows.” [02-c3]

For OT environments, that means the tool list and the context window are not implementation details; they are part of the control surface. [02-c3]

The same discipline should apply to beneficial claims about AI in grid operations and planning. [02-c4]

In a Utility Dive op-ed, an AWS energy lead argued that AI-powered grid tools can shorten approval studies from months to hours and run thousands of simulations while human engineers make the final decision. [02-c4]

That claim is plausible as an operational efficiency argument, but it is commercially interested because AWS has a direct stake in data-center and grid-AI adoption. [02-c4]

The counterweight is that AI-enabled grid planning sits inside wider disputes about reliability, infrastructure build-out, costs and siting. [02-c4]

In the CSIS discussion cited in this brief, Jasmine Sun described concerns about data-center electricity demand, new infrastructure, emissions, possible resident price effects and public trust around siting decisions. [02-c4]

My interpretation is that AI tools may help planners process studies faster, but they do not remove the need to test assumptions about who pays, who bears operational risk and whether the resulting infrastructure is reliable. [02-c4]

For executives, the right question is not whether a vendor can produce a recommendation quickly, but whether the operator can verify the model’s inputs, constraints, outputs and effect on engineering judgment. [02-c4]

Dragos’s warning deserves attention because it says its view is based on firsthand work with several frontier-model providers and non-public access to models. [02-c5]

Dragos also frames its own position as anti-hype, saying it has historically pushed back on claims such as phishing emails taking down “the power grid,” while saying that what it is now seeing does concern it. [02-c5]

That combination makes the warning relevant, but not conclusive. [02-c5]

It is still a source claim from a company with commercial incentives, not a public forensic record that lets operators independently test frequency, method and control failure. [02-c5]

The evidence therefore points to a middle position. [02-c1][02-c2][02-c3][02-c4][02-c5]

Dismissing AI risk as hype would ignore MITRE’s mapping of AI-specific techniques, Dragos’s account of AI-mediated OT discovery and IBM’s warning that agent context and tool scope can fail in predictable ways. [02-c1][02-c2][02-c3][02-c5]

Accepting every AI benefit or threat claim at face value would ignore the commercial incentives behind vendor narratives and the lack of full public incident detail. [02-c3][02-c4][02-c5]

The governance test should be whether a claim identifies a concrete operational pathway: access, tool use, data exfiltration, poisoned output, planning recommendation or grid-load behavior. [02-c1][02-c2][02-c3][02-c4]

The control test should be whether the operator can validate the pathway before deployment and monitor it during use. [02-c2][02-c3][02-c4]

What remains uncertain is how common these AI-assisted OT pathways are, how often agent failures produce operational consequences and how well today’s controls perform in live environments. [02-c1][02-c3][02-c5]

Until better public evidence exists, the prudent governance posture is neither panic nor dismissal, but disciplined restriction of agent access, tool scope, context quality and operational authority. [02-c1][02-c2][02-c3][02-c4][02-c5]

## What to watch

- Whether future ATT&CK updates and operator disclosures show more AI-assisted pathways reaching OT environments, rather than only enterprise IT systems. [02-c1][02-c2]

- Whether vendors provide verifiable evidence that AI grid-planning tools improve outcomes without shifting reliability, cost or siting risks onto others. [02-c4]

- Whether agent-governance controls such as tool curation, context pruning and output validation become auditable requirements rather than engineering preferences. [02-c3]
