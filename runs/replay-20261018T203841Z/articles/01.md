# The governance story moved from model policy to infrastructure control

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 1 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The important governance shift for critical-infrastructure leaders is not another abstract debate about model policy.  

It is that AI systems, AI data centres and automated analytical tools are being pulled into existing infrastructure-control regimes: access authorisation, cyber-system categorisation, reliability standards, physical resilience and local legitimacy. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c3] [01-c4] [01-c5]

That matters because agentic AI will not arrive only as a “model” to be assessed by an AI committee.  

It will arrive as an operational dependency, a user with access, a third-party service, a load on the grid, a facility in a community, or a control path into systems that already sit inside critical-infrastructure rules. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c4] [01-c5]

The clearest signal comes from the US bulk-power sector.  

Industrial Cyber reported that FERC’s 2026 CIP audit lessons flagged access-control risks as “super intelligence” tools, including chatbots, personal assistants and automated analytical systems, become more embedded in networks supporting critical infrastructure protection assets. [01-c1]

FERC staff’s reported lesson was practical: under CIP-004-7 requirements R4, R5 and R6, entities should ensure that access by those tools to BES Cyber Systems and BES Cyber System Information is authorised. [01-c1]

That is a governance move from “is this model safe?” to “is this tool an authorised access path into regulated cyber systems and regulated information?” [01-c1]

For executives, the operational implication is direct.  

If an AI assistant, analytics system or more autonomous agent can query, summarise, modify, route or expose BES Cyber System Information, it should be treated in access governance as more than ordinary software. [01-c1]

The relevant questions become familiar but harder: who approved the access, what identity does the tool use, what data can it reach, what logs exist, and what happens if its access is inherited through a vendor or affiliate. [01-c1] [01-c4]

This is where agentic AI changes the risk shape without requiring a speculative scenario.  

Industrial Cyber also reported FERC audit findings that, in two cases, systems individually categorised as low impact collectively met criteria for medium-impact classification. [01-c4]

The same report said entities did not adequately account for monitoring and control functions performed by affiliates and third-party contractors when categorising BES Cyber Systems. [01-c4]

That is not an AI incident report, but it is directly relevant to agentic governance because autonomous or semi-autonomous tools can aggregate visibility and influence across assets, owners and service relationships. [01-c4]

My interpretation is that classification based only on each owner’s local system view will miss some agentic-AI risks, especially where a shared tool monitors or controls multiple assets. [01-c4]

The second signal is load governance.  

Utility Dive reported that NERC stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centres, crypto mining facilities and other computational loads, moving those standards closer to finalisation. [01-c2]

Utility Dive also reported that NERC’s alert included seven actions grid entities “must implement to address immediate risks posed by computational loads” while permanent rules are developed. [01-c2]

The important point is not whether every AI data centre is itself an AI governance problem.  

The point is that large AI-related loads are being treated as bulk-power reliability issues, not only as commercial interconnection or economic-development projects. [01-c2]

For operators, that moves the governance workload into load modelling, communications with grid entities, operating assumptions and reliability planning. [01-c2]

This is infrastructure governance by another route: AI creates demand, demand creates reliability risk, and reliability risk creates obligations for grid-facing entities. [01-c2]

The third signal is resilience and concentration.  

The Atlantic Council argued, in a conflict-related analysis of attacks on Russian data centres, that digital data centres should be treated as critical infrastructure and receive physical protections comparable to strategic sites such as power plants and logistics hubs. [01-c3]

It also argued that data-storage backups and dispersal strategies should become routine features of digital policy. [01-c3]

That is a source’s argument, not evidence that comparable attack patterns will occur in Europe or the United States.  

Still, my interpretation is that the governance perimeter for AI data centres is expanding beyond cybersecurity and electricity demand to include physical protection, redundancy and concentration risk. [01-c3]

This is especially relevant where agentic systems depend on a small number of compute, storage or platform providers. [01-c3]

If those dependencies are operationally critical, leaders will need to ask whether backup, dispersal and recovery assumptions are verifiable rather than merely contractual. [01-c3]

The fourth signal is public legitimacy.  

On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun described local objections to data centres that included neighbourhood disruption, bright lights, construction effects, electricity use and affordability concerns. [01-c5]

She also described a process concern: residents and activists often encountered projects negotiated under nondisclosure arrangements, with limited visibility into what was being built, for whom, and under what terms. [01-c5]

My interpretation is that data-centre backlash should not be dismissed as a generic objection to AI. [01-c5]

For critical-infrastructure leaders, it is a warning that stakeholder process, siting transparency and distribution of local costs can become governance issues in their own right. [01-c5]

This matters for agentic AI because operational autonomy depends on infrastructure that must be powered, permitted, protected and socially accepted. [01-c2] [01-c5]

A control framework that covers model documentation but ignores grid impact, facility siting and local process will not cover the actual operating environment. [01-c2] [01-c5]

There are limits to this reading.  

The evidence here is strongest for the US electricity sector and AI data centres.  

It is weaker for Europe, water, telecoms and transport, and it includes limited direct testimony from operators.  

The Atlantic Council piece is conflict-related analysis, so it should be used as a resilience analogy, not as proof of likely attacks or outage patterns in Europe or the US.  

There is also an important definitional gap.  

FERC’s reported audit lessons refer to “SI” tools such as chatbots, personal assistants and automated analytical systems, not necessarily fully agentic systems that plan and act independently. [01-c1]

But the governance direction is still visible: as tools become embedded in operational networks, regulators and reliability bodies are likely to treat them through existing infrastructure obligations before any neat “agentic AI” checklist appears. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c4]

The practical conclusion is therefore conservative.  

Critical-infrastructure operators should not wait for a standalone agentic-AI rulebook.  

They should map where AI tools have access to regulated systems and information, where third parties or affiliates create shared control paths, where AI-related computational loads affect reliability assumptions, and where data-centre dependencies create resilience or legitimacy exposure. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c3] [01-c4] [01-c5]

The governance story has moved from model policy to infrastructure control. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c3] [01-c4] [01-c5]

## What to watch

- Whether final NERC computational-load standards convert today’s interim actions into durable reliability obligations for AI data centres and similar loads. [01-c2]

- Whether FERC and NERC treatment of AI or “SI” tools becomes more specific on identity, authorisation, logging and third-party access to BES Cyber Systems and BES Cyber System Information. [01-c1] [01-c4]

- Whether local data-centre opposition forces operators and customers to treat siting transparency, electricity impacts and community process as formal governance requirements rather than public-affairs issues. [01-c5]
