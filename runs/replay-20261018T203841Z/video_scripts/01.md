# The governance story moved from model policy to infrastructure control

[VISUAL: Heading: “AI governance is becoming infrastructure governance”]

What if the biggest AI governance change is not a new AI law, but the way existing infrastructure rules start treating AI as an access path, a load, and an operational dependency?

That is the shift this week’s evidence points to.

For critical-infrastructure leaders, the practical work is moving from abstract model policy to familiar control regimes.

Access authorization.

Asset categorization.

Reliability planning.

Physical resilience.

Local legitimacy.

The point is not that every AI system is suddenly safety-critical.

The point is that autonomous and AI-enabled systems are being pulled into the same governance machinery that already controls electricity, cyber access, and infrastructure risk.

[VISUAL: Quote: “Access by SI tools… should be authorized” — Industrial Cyber / FERC]

First, access control.

Industrial Cyber reported on the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s 2026 Critical Infrastructure Protection audit lessons for the United States power sector.

FERC staff highlighted risks from “super intelligence” tools, including chatbots, personal assistants, and automated analytical systems, as they become embedded in networks supporting critical infrastructure protection assets. [01-c1]

The key governance point is simple.

If an AI-enabled tool can reach Bulk Electric System Cyber Systems, or information about those systems, then it becomes an access-control issue. [01-c1]

Industrial Cyber reports that, under Critical Infrastructure Protection standard 004, requirements 4, 5, and 6, entities should ensure that access by these tools to Bulk Electric System Cyber Systems and Bulk Electric System Cyber System Information is authorized. [01-c1]

That is not a generic AI ethics obligation.

It is a power-sector compliance control.

For agentic AI, this matters because an agent that can retrieve information, trigger workflows, or assist operators may become part of the access pathway.

The governance question becomes less, “Is this model safe in general?”

It becomes, “What can this system touch, who approved that access, and how is that access revoked?”

[VISUAL: Heading: “Shared control can change asset risk” — Industrial Cyber / FERC]

Second, categorization and third-party control.

Industrial Cyber also reported that FERC audit findings identified cases where systems individually categorized as low impact collectively met criteria for medium-impact classification. [01-c4]

FERC staff also found that entities did not adequately account for monitoring and control functions performed by affiliates and third-party contractors when categorizing Bulk Electric System Cyber Systems. [01-c4]

This is important for agentic AI, even where the audit finding is not only about AI.

Here is our interpretation.

Agentic systems can concentrate control across assets, vendors, affiliates, and operational domains.

A tool that looks low risk inside one owner’s boundary may create higher aggregate risk when it monitors, recommends, or acts across many systems.

That is the governance gap.

Critical-infrastructure operators should not assess agentic AI only asset by asset.

They should also ask where shared control, outsourced monitoring, or third-party automation changes the effective impact category.

[VISUAL: Quote: “New reliability standards for AI data centers” — Utility Dive / NERC]

Third, load is becoming governance.

Utility Dive reported that North American Electric Reliability Corporation stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centers, crypto mining facilities, and other computational loads. [01-c2]

That is a step toward finalization, according to the grid watchdog, and it moves large AI data centers from a commercial interconnection issue toward a bulk-power reliability issue. [01-c2]

Utility Dive also reported that a NERC alert included seven actions that grid entities “must implement” to address immediate risks from computational loads while permanent rules are created. [01-c2]

The lesson is broader than data centers.

When AI becomes a large operational dependency, its infrastructure footprint becomes governable.

Power demand.

Load modeling.

Interconnection behavior.

Communications with grid entities.

These are not side issues.

They are part of the control environment around AI.

[VISUAL: Heading: “Data centers are also physical and political infrastructure” — Atlantic Council / CSIS]

Fourth, data centers are being governed as places, not just as networks.

The Atlantic Council argued, in conflict-related analysis about attacks on Russian data centers, that digital data centers should be treated as critical infrastructure and given physical protections comparable to other strategic sites, such as power plants and logistics hubs. [01-c3]

It also argued that backup and dispersal strategies should become routine features of digital policy. [01-c3]

This is analysis, not proof that similar attacks or outage patterns will occur in Europe or the United States.

But our interpretation is that data-center governance is broadening to include physical resilience and concentration risk, not only cybersecurity and energy demand. [01-c3]

There is also a public legitimacy issue.

On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun described local concerns about traffic, lights, construction nuisance, electricity use, affordability, and water. [01-c5]

She also described frustration with opaque local processes, including deals arranged under non-disclosure agreements before residents knew what project was coming. [01-c5]

Our interpretation is that local resistance to AI data centers should be read as an infrastructure-governance signal, not just a cultural objection to AI. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Heading: “What to watch: the checklist will not say ‘agentic AI’”]

So what should leaders watch?

Watch where AI appears inside existing control regimes.

In the United States electricity sector, that means Critical Infrastructure Protection access management, Bulk Electric System cyber-system categorization, computational-load standards, and reliability alerts.

For data centers, watch physical resilience, backup and dispersal, grid connection, and local approval processes.

For Europe, water, telecom, and transport, the evidence is thinner.

But the direction is still useful.

The near-term governance workload may not arrive as a clean “agentic AI” checklist.

It may arrive as authorization records, asset classifications, vendor-control reviews, load studies, resilience plans, and stakeholder processes.

Treat agentic AI as an operational dependency and an access pathway.

Not only as software.
