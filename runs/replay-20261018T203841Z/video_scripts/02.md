# The credible risk is AI-mediated access and acceleration, not magic autonomy

[VISUAL: Title slide — “AI in critical infrastructure: pathway, not panic”]

What is the real risk when artificial intelligence starts helping people act inside critical infrastructure?

[VISUAL: Slide — “1. The strongest public case is cross-domain assistance” — Source: MITRE and Dragos]

The clearest public evidence is not a fully autonomous artificial intelligence attack taking over operational technology, or OT, by itself.

It is narrower than that.

MITRE’s recent ATT&CK framework update, as reported by Industrial Cyber, added an artificial intelligence-assisted breach of Mexican government infrastructure that crossed from information technology, or IT, into OT context, reaching a municipal water utility. [02-c1]

Dragos described the same case from its incident-response perspective.

Dragos said the adversary was focused on the enterprise IT environment, with no OT skills, no OT knowledge, and no OT desire. [02-c1]

Then, according to Dragos, the artificial intelligence model identified a system as an interface down to the water utility’s OT networks and suggested that OT was a valuable target. [02-c1]

That matters because the risk pathway is concrete.

The model did not need to operate pumps or breakers.

It helped connect enterprise access to operational consequence.

Dragos’s warning deserves attention because the company says it has firsthand exposure to non-public frontier-model work and government discussions. [02-c5]

But this is still a company source claim, not independently published forensic evidence with full detail. [02-c5]

[VISUAL: Slide — “2. The threat model is becoming operational” — Source: MITRE ATT&CK]

MITRE’s update also shows that artificial intelligence-specific attack and defense issues are no longer only abstract concerns.

Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE added techniques for prompt injection, artificial intelligence-enabled data exfiltration, artificial intelligence result poisoning, and attacks on artificial intelligence-assisted defenses. [02-c2]

The same report said the release includes 15 new detection strategies and 38 analytics across enterprise and industrial control system environments. [02-c2]

For leaders, that is the governance signal.

If a risk can be mapped to a technique and a detection strategy, it can also be assigned an owner.

It can be tested.

It can be monitored.

And it can be limited through access control, tool permissions, logging, and validation.

The credible claim is not that artificial intelligence creates magic autonomy.

The credible claim is that it can accelerate a human attacker, confuse a defender, expose data, or turn an overlooked interface into an operational pathway. [02-c2]

[VISUAL: Slide — “3. Agents fail inside their own context” — Source: IBM]

IBM’s technical warning about artificial intelligence agents points to a related problem.

It is useful, but it should be treated as vendor interpretation.

IBM argues that large context windows still have a limited attention budget, and that agents can fail through context poisoning, distraction, confusion, and clash. [02-c3]

IBM also cites a benchmark where a model failed a task when offered 46 tools, but passed the same task when offered 19 tools. [02-c3]

The governance lesson is direct.

More context is not always safer.

More tools are not always better.

IBM’s proposed controls are validation, compaction, curation, and pruning. [02-c3]

In operational environments, that translates into hard questions.

Which tools can the agent call?

Which data can it read?

Which recommendations require human approval?

And which outputs must be tested before they affect operations?

[VISUAL: Slide — “4. AI may help planning, but the grid still has constraints” — Sources: AWS and CSIS]

Artificial intelligence is also being promoted as a tool for infrastructure planning.

In Utility Dive, an AWS energy lead claimed that artificial intelligence-powered grid tools can shorten approval studies from months to hours. [02-c4]

The same AWS argument says these systems run thousands of simulations and generate recommendations, while human engineers make the final decision. [02-c4]

That is plausible.

But it is also a commercially interested claim from a cloud provider with a stake in grid and data-center growth. [02-c4]

The CSIS artificial intelligence policy podcast adds the counterweight.

Its discussion of data-center backlash points to new electrical infrastructure, possible new gas plants, emissions, construction, and cost concerns for residents if companies do not cover rate impacts. [02-c4]

So the test is not whether artificial intelligence can produce a planning output.

The test is whether that output survives reliability review, cost-allocation scrutiny, siting politics, and operational validation. [02-c4]

[VISUAL: Closing slide — “What to watch: access, tools, validation, monitoring”]

What to watch now is evidence of operational effect.

Not slogans about autonomous systems.

Look for claims that identify a pathway: access, tool use, data exfiltration, poisoned results, planning recommendations, or grid-load behavior.

Then ask whether the operator can verify it.

Can they see what the agent saw?

Can they limit what it can do?

Can they test its output before it changes operations?

Can they detect when artificial intelligence helps an attacker move from IT into OT?

That is where governance should focus.
