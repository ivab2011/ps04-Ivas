# What operators should do now: govern agents as accounts, vendors, loads, and dependencies

[VISUAL: Title slide. Heading: “Do not wait for a perfect agentic-AI framework.” Source: Agentic GRC]

What should critical-infrastructure operators do now, before agentic artificial intelligence gets its own perfect rulebook?

## 1. Treat agents as accounts

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “AI tools need named access.” Short quote: “authorization, account, BCSI, communications.” Source: Industrial Cyber / Ampyx Cyber]

Start with the controls you already have.

In the United States power sector, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission has flagged risks from artificial-intelligence tools and third-party control systems. Industrial Cyber reported that entities must control, regularly review, and include system-integrator access in existing risk-management frameworks. [03-c1]

Ampyx Cyber, a company interpreting Federal Energy Regulatory Commission audit lessons, goes further. It says that for each artificial-intelligence tool, operators should be able to show the Critical Infrastructure Protection authorization, the account it runs under, the Bulk Electric System Cyber System Information it can reach, and the communications it makes outside the Electronic Security Perimeter. [03-c1]

That is a practical governance move. Do not start by debating whether the tool is “agentic enough.” Start by asking whether it has identity, permissions, data reach, and external communications that someone can approve and monitor.

## 2. Treat AI features as change events

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “AI can arrive after purchase.” Short quote: “features that can be switched on after purchase.” Source: Industrial Cyber / Ampyx Cyber]

The next control point is procurement and change management.

Ampyx Cyber says operators should keep vendor documentation on artificial-intelligence features, including features that can be switched on after purchase. [03-c3]

My interpretation is that this turns an artificial-intelligence feature into a change event, not just a product capability. Procurement, security, operations, and compliance teams need to know when a dormant function becomes active, who enabled it, what data it can see, and what actions it can take. [03-c3]

IBM, in a company video on context engineering for artificial-intelligence agents, argues that agents should not receive every tool or every piece of context by default. It says the fix for context confusion is curation: give the agent only the handful of tools the job calls for. [03-c3]

IBM also says outdated or poisoned information should be pruned, or the agent should start with a fresh context window that restates the current corrected state. [03-c3]

For operators, the governance implication is clear. Constrain the tools. Constrain the context. Validate what is saved. Treat the agent’s working environment as part of the control surface.

## 3. Treat data centers as grid loads and critical sites

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “Computational load becomes a reliability issue.” Key number: “50 megawatts at 100 kilovolts.” Source: Utility Dive / NERC Project 2026-02]

Artificial intelligence is not only software inside the control room. It is also load on the grid.

Utility Dive reported that proposed North American Electric Reliability Corporation standards for computational loads would cover interconnection studies and modeling, operational data and communications, and load protection coordination and disturbance monitoring. [03-c2]

The same report says the standards would apply to computational loads greater than or equal to 50 megawatts, connected at voltage levels greater than or equal to 100 kilovolts. [03-c2]

So operators and large-load customers should watch the North American Electric Reliability Corporation board process and the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission approval path. These rules could turn artificial-intelligence data centers and similar large sites into explicit reliability-planning objects. [03-c2]

There is also a physical-resilience angle. The Atlantic Council, interpreting recent Ukrainian strikes on Russian data centers, argued that digital data centers should be treated as critical infrastructure and given physical protections like other strategic sites. [03-c4]

It also argued that backup and dispersal strategies should be routine features of digital policy. [03-c4]

My interpretation is that physical protection, backup, and dispersal are governance controls, not only engineering choices. [03-c4]

## 4. Treat legitimacy as a risk control

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “Backlash is often about process.” Short quote: “AI rarely came up.” Source: CSIS AI Policy Podcast]

Data-center governance is also about legitimacy.

On the Center for Strategic and International Studies AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun said that when she spoke with people about data centers, artificial intelligence rarely came up. Their concerns were more often about infrastructure, quality-of-life effects, corporate distrust, and backroom deals. [03-c6]

The same podcast discussed electricity demand, possible new power infrastructure, emissions, construction, and possible electricity price increases if companies do not cover resulting rate hikes. [03-c6]

It also described local frustration when data-center deals were arranged under nondisclosure agreements, leaving communities unsure what project was coming, who the customer was, and what the site would be used for. [03-c6]

My interpretation is that transparency about electricity impacts, cost allocation, and community benefits should be treated as a risk control. Backlash may come from infrastructure and process concerns, not simply opposition to artificial intelligence. [03-c6]

## What to watch

[VISUAL: Closing slide. Heading: “Watchlist.” Phrases: “NERC/FERC, vendor AI features, migration playbooks.” Sources: Utility Dive, Industrial Cyber, Singapore CSA]

Three signals matter next.

First, watch whether the North American Electric Reliability Corporation and the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission finalize computational-load standards in a form that creates concrete duties for large sites. [03-c2]

Second, watch vendor-enabled artificial-intelligence features, especially functions that can be switched on after purchase. [03-c3]

Third, watch whether regulators copy migration-style playbooks from other emerging-technology risks. Singapore’s quantum-safe program is not artificial-intelligence regulation. It is only an analogy. But Industrial Cyber reported that Singapore’s Cyber Security Agency guidance recommends migration plans by 2027, completion by 2035, earlier migration for Critical Information Infrastructure by 2031, and reporting from pilot participants on findings and demonstrated impact. [03-c5]

That playbook converts uncertainty into inventories, owners, dates, pilots, and evidence of readiness. Artificial-intelligence governance may move in the same direction. [03-c5]
