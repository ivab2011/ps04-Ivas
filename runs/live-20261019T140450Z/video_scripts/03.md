# Build an agent-access register before the rules harden

[VISUAL: Hook slide. Heading: “Can you evidence the agent?” Simple diagram: AI tool → account → data → external communication → human review.]

## Hook

If an artificial intelligence, or AI, agent acted inside your operational environment tomorrow, could you prove who approved it, what account it used, what data it touched, and when a human reviewed it?

That is the management issue now.

Not a future agentic-AI rulebook.

Not a theoretical debate about autonomy.

The practical move is to extend controls you already have into an agent-access register.

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “Audit lesson: treat AI as access.” Quote: “authorization, account, information access, communications.” Source: Ampyx Cyber on FERC.]

## 1. The audit lesson is about evidence

Ampyx Cyber, interpreting Federal Energy Regulatory Commission audit lessons, says operators should evidence each AI tool’s Critical Infrastructure Protection authorization, account, sensitive Bulk Electric System cyber information access, and communications outside the electronic security perimeter. [03-c1]

Ampyx also says treating an AI tool as a generic account puts it into account-management processes power-sector entities already run. [03-c1]

This is not a dedicated agentic-AI compliance regime; it shows AI being pulled into familiar evidence categories: authorization, account ownership, information access, and monitored communications. [03-c1]

Procurement follows.

Ampyx says entities should know which protected-environment products have AI features today, including vendor-enabled features after purchase. [03-c2]

FERC also flagged that some tools may mimic user behavior, making legitimate activity harder to distinguish from malicious use and complicating detection and response. [03-c2]

So procurement and supply-chain reviews should ask not only what capability is advertised now, but what can be switched on later. [03-c2]

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “Agent governance: beyond logs.” Quote: “goal, intent, output review.” Source: IBM.]

## 2. Agents need intent evidence, not just logs

IBM, speaking as a technology vendor, frames agent control as more than recording tool use.

In its “loop engineering” explanation, IBM says an agent goal should be specific and verifiable, with a clear stopping condition. [03-c4]

IBM also says humans need to write down the intent, keep it current, and make it visible to the agent. [03-c4]

And IBM says the human role includes reading and understanding generated AI output. [03-c4]

That company claim is useful for governance: for agentic systems, the register should record business purpose, measurable goal, written intent, human review requirement, and the owner accountable for keeping them current. [03-c4]

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “AI infrastructure is also local infrastructure.” Quote: “electricity, construction, transparency.” Source: CSIS AI Policy Podcast.]

## 3. The operating model reaches outside the control room

The Center for Strategic and International Studies podcast discussion on data-center backlash makes a different point.

Community concerns are not only about AI models; they include quality of life, construction disruption, environmental impact, electricity infrastructure, possible resident rate increases, and opaque decision-making. [03-c5]

For critical-infrastructure leaders, AI infrastructure governance is not only cyber governance; it also has to address electricity demand, construction effects, affordability, and decision transparency. [03-c5]

That matters where large AI data centers become major grid loads.

Utility Dive reports that the North American Electric Reliability Corporation’s Project 2026-02 includes proposed standards for computational loads, covering interconnection studies and modeling, operational data and communications, and load protection coordination and disturbance monitoring. [03-c3]

Utility Dive also reports that the North American Electric Reliability Corporation board is expected to consider those standards in the fourth quarter. [03-c3]

[VISUAL: Closing slide. Heading: “What to watch.” Short quote: “Exposure first. Deadlines later.” Source: Singapore quantum-safe advisory and FERC lessons.]

## What to watch

AI governance may follow a pattern already visible in quantum-safe migration.

In that precedent, Singapore’s advisory asks exposed organizations to put a migration plan in place by 2027, aim to complete migration by 2035, and establish clear ownership. [03-c6]

Used only as an analogy, the pattern is: identify exposure, assign ownership, engage vendors, plan staged migration, and prepare evidence before deadlines harden. [03-c6]

FERC’s AI-related lessons already point in that direction for business need, authorization, access review, external communications, generic service-account inventory, and vendor risk assessment. [03-c6]

The open question is whether regulators will require a distinct AI inventory, or keep embedding AI expectations into existing access, supply-chain, reliability, and audit controls.

Either way, the near-term executive action is the same.

Build one agent-access register that can serve auditors, reliability planners, boards, and external stakeholders.
