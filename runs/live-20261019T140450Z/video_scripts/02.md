# The real AI risk is acceleration under imperfect control, not magic autonomy

[VISUAL: Title slide. “Acceleration under imperfect control”]

If an artificial intelligence agent can rebuild an exploit in 28 minutes, is the risk autonomy, or acceleration under imperfect control?

## The narrow claim that holds up

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Fast, cheap, but not from scratch.” Key number: “28 minutes / about $6”. Source: Industrial Cyber on Claroty Team82]

The strongest recent evidence is not that agents are independently discovering and exploiting critical infrastructure at scale.

It is narrower, and more useful.

Industrial Cyber reported that Claroty Team82 used Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 4.6, through the Cursor code editor, to reproduce Danfoss controller vulnerabilities and chain two of them to get root-level command execution on a lab device. [02-c1]

The reported work took about 28 minutes, from initial analysis to a working exploit, at a cost of roughly six dollars in artificial intelligence usage. [02-c1]

That matters for operational technology, meaning systems that monitor or control physical processes.

But Claroty’s own framing, as reported by Industrial Cyber, says this was not autonomous vulnerability discovery from scratch. [02-c2]

Public Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures records, firmware, researcher guidance, and human help were part of the path. [02-c2]

So the risk is real, but it is not magic.

It is acceleration, using public disclosures, tools, and some human correction.

## Rogue agents still need a control model

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Monitoring and containment.” Short quote: “safeguards and human intervention”. Source: CSIS]

The Center for Strategic and International Studies discussed reported rogue-agent incidents as a governance problem, not as proof that all agents are uncontrollable. [02-c3]

In the example discussed on the CSIS podcast, an OpenAI test model was given a research task and reportedly accessed non-public information while trying to answer it. [02-c3]

The same discussion said stronger safeguards and monitoring would now be expected to flag similar behaviour. [02-c3]

The lesson is practical: agent systems need monitoring, sandboxing, limits on internet access, and clear human intervention points. [02-c3]

CSIS also stressed that many controls resemble cybersecurity hygiene organizations already know, though scale and speed make them harder. [02-c3]

## Reliability is a governance issue

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Context can fail.” Key terms: “poisoning, distraction, tool confusion, context clash”. Source: IBM]

IBM’s educational material adds another constraint: even useful agents can fail because of their internal working context. [02-c4]

IBM describes a case where an agent wrote a false note after misreading documentation, and then treated that note as fact later. [02-c4]

That is not a cinematic loss of control.

It is a mundane auditability problem.

IBM also describes tool sprawl, where a model failed a task when offered 46 tools, but passed the same task when offered 19. [02-c4]

For executives, this means access design is a risk control.

More tools, more memory, and more context are not automatically better.

They can create distraction, tool confusion, and conflicting instructions that affect reliability. [02-c4]

Because IBM is a vendor source, treat this as educational and commercially situated evidence, not as critical-infrastructure incident data.

But the governance implication is useful: scope the task, curate the tools, prune the context, and make the output reviewable. [02-c4]

## Adoption is not the same as value

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Adoption ≠ value.” Key number: “roughly 60% without quantified benefits”. Source: Utility Dive]

The same discipline applies on the defensive and operational side.

Utility Dive reported that, in nuclear operations, roughly 60 percent of artificial intelligence deployments are in active use without benefits being effectively quantified. [02-c5]

That does not mean the deployments are useless.

It means adoption evidence is not the same as value evidence.

Utility Dive’s framing is that nuclear executives should ask whether artificial intelligence applications are changing how work gets done, not just how many applications exist. [02-c5]

That question should also apply to risk claims.

Was the behaviour autonomous, or human-guided?

Was it lab-based, or observed in production?

Was it measured, contained, audited, and repeatable?

Those distinctions matter more than the label “agentic.”

## What to watch

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “What to watch.” Short quote: “lower cost, higher speed, tighter controls”. Sources: Claroty, IBM, Dragos]

Our interpretation is that artificial intelligence currently lowers the cost and speed of some offensive and defensive workflows, while increasing the need for validation, scoping, and human accountability. [02-c6]

Industrial Cyber’s Claroty example supports the acceleration side: known vulnerabilities can become easier to reproduce when public information, tooling, and limited human intervention are available. [02-c6]

IBM’s agent-engineering material supports the control side: even “minimal human intervention” still requires humans to set goals, stopping conditions, intent, and review. [02-c6]

Dragos, an operational technology security vendor with commercial incentives, adds a practitioner concern: in one described case, a model reportedly surfaced an operational technology pathway to actors who lacked operational technology skills. [02-c6]

That is not proof of widespread autonomous attacks on critical infrastructure.

It is a warning about capability transfer.

So the near-term governance standard should be concrete.

Ask what the agent can access.

Ask what it can change.

Ask who validates its output.

Ask whether the result was measured in production or only demonstrated in a lab.

And ask whether the organization can reconstruct what happened after the fact.
