# AI governance moved from principles to operational scoping

*As of 2026-10-19. Part 1 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The practical shift in AI governance for critical infrastructure is not that AI has become a wholly separate risk category. [01-c5]  
It is that regulators, standards bodies and threat frameworks are placing AI into existing operational governance mechanisms: authorised access, asset evaluation, load modelling, communications protocols, detection engineering and public accountability. [01-c5]  

That matters for agentic AI because autonomy becomes governable only when tied to a system boundary, access path, operational dependency or monitored behaviour. [01-c5]  
For executives, the near-term question is less “Do we have an AI policy?” and more “Where can AI act, what can it reach, what load does it create, what assumptions does it change, and who can verify the answer?” [01-c5]  

The first signal comes from FERC’s 2026 CIP audit lessons, as reported by Industrial Cyber. [01-c1]  
FERC’s lesson is concrete: under CIP-004-7 requirements R4, R5 and R6, entities should ensure that access by SI tools to BES Cyber Systems and BES Cyber System Information is authorised. [01-c1]  
That places AI-related tooling inside access governance rather than treating it as an abstract model-risk problem. [01-c1]  

The important point is not that the CIP Reliability Standards now contain a comprehensive AI regime. [01-c5]  
Industrial Cyber reported that the standards neither explicitly allow nor prohibit SI tools, but entities should consider the risks those tools pose when evaluating BES Cyber Systems and apply appropriate controls. [01-c5]  
In my interpretation, AI is being translated into questions auditors already know how to ask about authorisation, system categorisation and control application. [01-c5]  

The second signal comes from NERC’s treatment of AI data centres and other computational loads. [01-c2]  
Utility Dive reported that NERC stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centers, crypto mining facilities and other computational loads, moving them closer to finalisation. [01-c2]  
The reported scope is operational: the standards would apply to computational loads of at least 50 MW connected at voltage levels of at least 100 kV. [01-c2]  

This turns AI infrastructure into a bulk-power reliability governance issue, not just a siting, sustainability or energy-procurement question. [01-c2]  
Utility Dive reported that the standards are designed to ensure transmission operators, balancing authorities and reliability coordinators have computational-load site data needed to plan, monitor and assess reliable operation of the bulk power system. [01-c5]  
The same reporting says the standards would establish communication capabilities with predefined protocols so operators can take prompt action to prevent or mitigate events that adversely affect bulk-power reliability. [01-c5]  

For critical-infrastructure leaders, this is a different kind of AI risk than model hallucination or unsafe prompt behaviour. [01-c2]  
The AI system may be elsewhere, but the data-centre load, interconnection behaviour and communications requirements become part of the operator’s reliability model. [01-c2]  
In my interpretation, this is AI governance by grid behaviour: the control point is not the model weights, but the demand pattern and the operator’s ability to see and coordinate around it. [01-c5]  

The third signal comes from MITRE ATT&CK v20. [01-c3]  
Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE released version 20 of ATT&CK with expanded coverage of critical infrastructure threats, AI-enabled attacks and cyber espionage. [01-c3]  
The same report says Enterprise gained AI-focused and AI-enabled techniques, while ICS added techniques for command and script execution and command and control over protocols already used by industrial networks. [01-c3]  

This does not prove that every operator is facing novel AI-driven intrusions today. [01-c3]  
It does show that AI-enabled activity is being mapped into defender workflows across enterprise and industrial-control environments. [01-c3]  
In governance terms, security leaders should review whether existing detection engineering, logging and response assumptions still match the behaviours now being catalogued. [01-c3]  

The fourth signal is less technical but still material. [01-c4]  
In a CSIS AI Policy Podcast episode, Jasmine Sun argued that some industry responses to data-centre backlash dismiss local concerns too quickly by treating water-use fears as misinformation and then extrapolating that the whole backlash is overblown. [01-c4]  
Sun’s claim is not a regulator finding, but it is relevant because AI infrastructure is experienced locally as electricity demand, construction, emissions, pollution, noise, land-use change and negotiated public risk. [01-c4]  

Sun also argued that concerns about electricity prices, behind-the-meter gas plants, emissions and pollution can be valid even where specific water-use claims are overstated. [01-c4]  
She further described a trust problem, where communities encounter data-centre projects through opaque deals, nondisclosure agreements and negotiations that feel undemocratic. [01-c4]  
For operators and their advisers, community resistance should be treated as a signal about infrastructure impact and legitimacy, not merely as a communications problem. [01-c4]  

Taken together, these developments point in the same direction. [01-c5]  
FERC places AI-related tools inside access control and BES Cyber System evaluation. [01-c1]  
NERC places AI-driven computational load inside planning, monitoring and reliability communications. [01-c2]  
MITRE places AI-enabled adversary behaviour inside defender mapping and detection workflows. [01-c3]  
CSIS’s podcast discussion places data-centre backlash inside the governance of trust, local impact and accountability. [01-c4]  

The counterargument is that none of this yet amounts to a single agentic-AI governance framework for critical infrastructure. [01-c5]  
That counterargument is correct. [01-c5]  
The evidence here is strongest for the U.S. power sector and AI data-centre load integration, and weaker for European implementation or for detailed evidence of operators’ internal AI governance practices. [01-c1][01-c2]  
Claims about AI-enabled adversary behaviour are also partly mediated through threat-framework and trade-press interpretation rather than primary incident files. [01-c3]  

The remaining uncertainty is timing and visibility. [01-c5]  
It is not yet clear how quickly these expectations will become enforceable requirements, or whether operators can reliably identify AI features embedded by vendors after procurement. [01-c5]  
But waiting for a complete AI rulebook would miss the point of the current shift. [01-c5]  
Governance is already moving into operational scoping, where the required evidence is who or what has access, what the asset does, what the grid must model, what defenders must detect and what affected communities can verify. [01-c5]  

## What to watch

- Whether FERC audit expectations around AI-related tools become more explicit in future CIP guidance or enforcement practice. [01-c1][01-c5]  
- Whether NERC’s computational-load standards are finalised in a form that gives operators usable site data and communication protocols. [01-c2][01-c5]  
- Whether ATT&CK’s AI-enabled techniques translate into measurable changes in OT detection engineering and incident response testing. [01-c3]
