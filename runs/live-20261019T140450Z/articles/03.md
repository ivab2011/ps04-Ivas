# Build an agent-access register before the rules harden

*As of 2026-10-19. Part 3 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

Critical-infrastructure operators should not wait for a dedicated agentic-AI rulebook before tightening governance. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4]  
The practical move now is to extend existing access, supply-chain, monitoring and review controls into an agent-access register covering every AI feature, agent, assistant or embedded tool. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4]  
That register should identify an owner, business purpose, account model, data reach, external communications, vendor assessment, monitoring plan and human review requirement. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4]

This is my interpretation of the combined evidence, not a new regulatory requirement. [03-c6]  
The evidence points in one direction: AI governance is likely to harden first through existing audit, access-control, supply-chain and reliability mechanisms, rather than through a clean, separate “agentic AI” compliance regime. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c3] [03-c6]

The clearest near-term signal comes from the U.S. power sector. [03-c1] [03-c2]  
Ampyx Cyber, interpreting FERC’s 2026 CIP audit lessons, says entities should be able to show, for each AI tool, the CIP-004 authorization, the account it runs under, the bulk electric system cyber system information it can reach, and the communications it makes outside the electronic security perimeter. [03-c1]  
Ampyx also argues that treating an AI tool as a generic account places it inside an account-management process that registered entities already operate. [03-c1]

That is not yet a universal agentic-AI statute, but it is an important management clue. [03-c1]  
If an AI assistant can query sensitive information, act under an account, communicate outside a protected environment or influence operational workflows, the governance question becomes less “is this AI?” and more “can we evidence authorization, access, monitoring and review?” [03-c1] [03-c4]

Procurement is the second pressure point. [03-c2]  
Ampyx says entities should know which products in their CIP-networked environment have AI features today, including features enabled by a vendor after purchase. [03-c2]  
Industrial Cyber’s report on FERC’s findings also says AI or security intelligence tools may mimic user behavior, making it harder to distinguish legitimate activity from malicious use and complicating detection and response. [03-c2]  
For executives, vendor review cannot stop at the product as bought; it has to cover AI functions that may be activated, updated or embedded later. [03-c2]

The agent-access register should therefore connect procurement to operations. [03-c1] [03-c2]  
For each tool, the operator should be able to answer who approved it, what business need it serves, which account it uses, what sensitive information it can reach, whether it communicates externally, which vendor controls apply, and how activity is monitored. [03-c1] [03-c2]  
That is a governance artefact boards can understand, auditors can test and security teams can maintain. [03-c1] [03-c2]

Agentic systems add another layer. [03-c4]  
IBM, in vendor educational content, frames effective AI-agent design around measurable goals, explicit intent and human review of the output. [03-c4]  
Its examples are not critical-infrastructure audit evidence, and IBM has a commercial interest in the topic. [03-c4]  
Still, the control lesson is useful: a log showing that an agent used a tool is not enough if the goal was vague, the business intent was absent or no human checked the output. [03-c4]

For critical infrastructure, agent governance should record more than access events. [03-c4]  
It should record the agent’s purpose, stopping condition, constraints, escalation points and review requirement. [03-c4]  
If an agent optimizes for a narrow technical objective while undermining a broader operational or business objective, the failure may not appear as unauthorized access; it may appear as authorized automation pursuing the wrong goal. [03-c4]

The same evidence system also has to serve reliability planners. [03-c3]  
Utility Dive reports that NERC Project 2026-02 includes proposed standards for computational loads, including requirements around interconnection studies and modelling, operational data and communications, and load protection coordination and disturbance monitoring. [03-c3]  
For operators exposed to large AI data-centre or computational-load projects, the watch item is not only cyber access but also whether modelling data, communication protocols and disturbance monitoring become enforceable evidence expectations. [03-c3]

AI infrastructure governance also widens beyond the control room. [03-c5]  
In a CSIS podcast, Jasmine Sun argues that data-centre backlash is not only about model risk or abstract AI fears. [03-c5]  
She describes local concerns around quality of life, construction, electricity infrastructure, emissions, possible resident price impacts and opaque decision-making processes. [03-c5]  
For infrastructure operators, community-facing governance should address electricity, construction, affordability and decision transparency, not only cybersecurity and model assurance. [03-c5]

There is a useful analogy from another emerging-technology risk, but it should be treated only as an analogy. [03-c6]  
Industrial Cyber reports that Singapore’s quantum-safe advisory recommends that exposed organizations put a migration plan in place by 2027 and aim to complete migration by 2035, and also identifies clear ownership as a step. [03-c6]  
That does not prove anything about AI systems, but it shows a governance pattern that could reappear: identify exposure, assign ownership, engage vendors, plan staged migration and build evidence before hard deadlines arrive. [03-c6]

The limitation is that this brief extrapolates from U.S. power-sector audit lessons and standards work to broader critical infrastructure. [03-c1] [03-c3]  
That extrapolation is reasonable for executives who operate across access control, supply chain, reliability and stakeholder-risk regimes, but it is not proven for water, transport, telecoms or European operators. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c3] [03-c5]  
Ampyx is a consultancy source interpreting FERC, IBM is vendor educational content, and CSIS is podcast-based policy interpretation. [03-c1] [03-c4] [03-c5]  
The remaining uncertainty is whether regulators will require a distinct AI inventory, or whether they will continue embedding AI expectations into existing access, supply-chain and reliability controls. [03-c6]

Either way, the management action is the same. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4]  
Build the agent-access register now, using controls the organization already understands. [03-c1] [03-c2]  
Then extend it to capture agent goals, intent, stopping conditions and human review. [03-c4]  
If the rules harden, the operator will already have the evidence system regulators are likely to ask for. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c3] [03-c6]

## What to watch

- Whether FERC, NERC or other regulators turn today’s AI access-control and vendor-risk lessons into explicit audit evidence expectations. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c6]  
- Whether NERC’s computational-load standards move forward and make modelling, operational communications and disturbance monitoring more concrete for AI data-centre projects. [03-c3]  
- Whether community opposition to AI infrastructure forces operators to evidence not only technical readiness, but also transparency around electricity, construction and affordability impacts. [03-c5]
