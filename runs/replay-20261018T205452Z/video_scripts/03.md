# What operators should do next: make AI governable before it becomes operationally normal

[VISUAL: Heading slide. “AI is becoming operational before AI law is finished.” Source: Agentic GRC]

## Hook

What should critical-infrastructure operators do now, before autonomous AI tools become normal parts of regulated operations?

[VISUAL: Slide with checklist title. “Make AI visible.” Quote: “features a vendor enabled after purchase.” Source: Industrial Cyber / Ampyx Cyber]

## 1. Start with inventory, not policy theater

The near-term answer is not to wait for a perfect AI-specific law.

It is to make existing governance, risk and compliance machinery work for AI.

Ampyx Cyber, speaking as a company source in Industrial Cyber, says entities should know which products in their NERC Critical Infrastructure Protection networked environment have AI features today, including features a vendor enabled after the product was purchased. [03-c1]

That matters because many operators will not first meet AI as a standalone system.

They will meet it as a feature inside monitoring tools, analytics platforms, maintenance software or vendor-managed products.

So the first evidence file is simple.

Show what AI is present, when it was enabled, whether procurement, security and operations approved it, and whether a vendor can change its behavior after deployment.

[VISUAL: Slide with evidence-file graphic. “Authorization. Account. Data reach. External communications.” Source: Industrial Cyber / Ampyx Cyber]

## 2. Turn AI use into auditable evidence

For regulated environments, the question is not only whether an AI tool is useful.

It is whether an auditor can inspect who authorized it, what account it runs under, what sensitive information it can reach, and where it communicates.

Ampyx Cyber says that, for each AI tool, entities should be able to show the Critical Infrastructure Protection dash zero zero four authorization, the account it runs under, the Bulk Electric System Cyber System Information it can reach, and the communications it makes outside the Electronic Security Perimeter. [03-c2]

That converts AI governance from a principle into a control record.

An agent is an identity, permissions, tools, and a pathway to external systems.

If those cannot be described, they cannot be governed.

[VISUAL: Slide with agent test screen. “Validate. Compact. Curate. Prune.” Source: IBM]

## 3. Test the failure modes agents actually have

Technical teams also need to test controls that reduce what an agent can see or use.

IBM, as a company source, describes four controls for agent context problems: validate conclusions against something real, compact long histories, curate the tools available to the agent, and prune conflicting or superseded state. [03-c6]

The important point is that all four controls limit the agent.

They do not make it more autonomous.

They make it more governable.

For critical infrastructure, test cases should include deliberate constraints: remove unnecessary tools, shorten context, force validation before action, clear stale instructions, and record whether the agent still performs safely.

[VISUAL: Slide with grid and data center icon. “50 megawatts. 100 kilovolts.” Source: Utility Dive]

## 4. Govern the external footprint too

AI governance does not stop at the control room.

Utility Dive reported that proposed reliability standards for computational loads would apply to loads greater than or equal to 50 megawatts, connected at voltage levels greater than or equal to 100 kilovolts. [03-c3]

Utility Dive also reported that the North American Electric Reliability Corporation board is expected to consider the new standards in the fourth quarter, based on development timelines in the proposals. [03-c3]

Executives should watch this because large AI data centers are becoming reliability events, not only real-estate projects.

Community legitimacy also belongs in governance.

On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun argued that data-center backlash should not be dismissed only as misinformation about water use. [03-c4]

She described concerns about electricity use, nuisance, local process, nondisclosure agreements, and whether communities receive a fair deal. [03-c4]

For operators and AI customers, local trust, electricity impacts and deal transparency are governance risks, not public-relations afterthoughts. [03-c4]

[VISUAL: Closing slide. “What to watch: rules, vendors, pilots, evidence.” Source: Industrial Cyber / Singapore quantum-safe guidance]

## What to watch

There is one useful analogy, but it has limits.

Industrial Cyber reported that Singapore’s quantum-safe guidance recommends exposure assessment, migration plans by 2027, completion aims by 2035, vendor engagement, and pilot reports showing learnings and demonstrated impact. [03-c5]

My interpretation is that AI governance may borrow this regulatory pattern: define exposure, assign owners, engage vendors, set deadlines, and require evidence from pilots. [03-c5]

But this is only an analogy.

It is not evidence that AI regulation will follow the same schedule or path.

The practical next step is immediate.

Prove what the AI can reach.

Prove what it can do.

Prove who authorized it.

Prove who can stop it.

And prove which external dependencies, grid impacts and communities it affects.
