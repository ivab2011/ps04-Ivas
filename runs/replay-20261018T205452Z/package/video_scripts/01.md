# AI governance has moved from policy debate to control evidence

[VISUAL: Heading slide — “From AI policy to control evidence”]

What changed when artificial intelligence started showing up inside critical-infrastructure governance?

The answer is not that autonomous agents are suddenly running the grid, water systems or transport networks. The shift is narrower: AI is being pulled into existing control frameworks — access control, asset categorization, detection logic, operational data and accountable communications. That is my interpretation of the evidence in this brief. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Quote slide — “SI tools… embedded in networks supporting CIP assets” — Industrial Cyber / FERC]

First, look at power-sector cyber compliance.

Industrial Cyber reported that Federal Energy Regulatory Commission staff flagged access-control risks from “super intelligence” tools, including chatbots, personal assistants and automated analytical systems, as those tools become embedded in networks supporting Critical Infrastructure Protection assets. [01-c1]

That matters because Critical Infrastructure Protection, or CIP, is not an abstract AI ethics framework. It is where bulk-power entities manage authorized access, monitoring, controls and evidence.

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission item does not say the CIP Reliability Standards explicitly allow or prohibit these tools. It says entities should consider the risks when evaluating Bulk Electric System Cyber Systems and apply appropriate controls. [01-c5]

So if an AI-like tool can touch a protected environment, it becomes part of the access-control and monitoring problem. [01-c1] It may also affect Bulk Electric System Cyber System categorization, if the tool changes risk to systems already governed under reliability rules. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Heading slide — “AI as grid load” / “NERC standards move closer” — Utility Dive]

Second, AI is appearing as infrastructure load.

Utility Dive reported that North American Electric Reliability Corporation stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centers, crypto-mining facilities and other large computational loads, moving those standards closer to finalization. [01-c2]

Here, the issue is not whether an AI model gives bad advice. The issue is whether computational loads affect reliable operation of the bulk power system. [01-c2]

The proposed standards are designed to give transmission operators, balancing authorities and reliability coordinators the computational-load site data they need to plan, monitor and assess reliable operation. [01-c5]

They also establish communication capabilities with predefined protocols, so operators can act promptly to prevent or mitigate events that could harm bulk-power reliability. [01-c5]

For executives, the reminder is simple: AI governance is not only about software behavior. It can also include siting, load visibility, communication paths and operational accountability. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Framework slide — “ATT&CK adds AI-assisted activity” — MITRE / Industrial Cyber]

Third, threat modeling is adapting.

Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE ATT&CK version 20 added campaigns covering attacks on more than 30 Minnesota water and wastewater utilities, and an AI-assisted breach of Mexican government infrastructure that crossed information technology and operational technology environments, reaching a municipal water utility. [01-c3]

The point is not that every infrastructure attack is now AI-driven. The point is that a major threat-modeling framework is beginning to represent AI-assisted activity across those boundaries. [01-c3]

Industrial Cyber also reported that MITRE expanded enterprise coverage with techniques for prompt injection, AI-enabled data exfiltration, AI result poisoning and attacks targeting AI-assisted defenses. [01-c5]

That turns AI from a general concern into something defenders can map into detection analytics and response planning. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Caution slide — “Company claim, not sector proof” — Dragos]

A practitioner view points in the same direction, with an important caveat.

In a Dragos video, the company said it had avoided treating AI in operational technology as real field activity until it saw authentic use cases, but now says the last nine months have changed its view. [01-c4]

That is a company claim from an operational-technology security vendor, not independent proof of systemic prevalence. [01-c4]

It is still useful as field interpretation. It suggests that, for some defenders, AI in operational-technology attack and defense workflows is no longer only conference talk. [01-c4]

But the visible governance response in the evidence is more concrete than broad “AI safety” language. It is about controlled actors, software features, detection problems and grid loads. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Closing slide — “What to watch: evidence, not slogans”]

What to watch next: whether these signals become auditable obligations.

For power operators, watch how Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and North American Electric Reliability Corporation expectations translate into control evidence around AI-like tools, protected systems, computational-load data and communication protocols. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c5]

For security leaders, watch whether MITRE’s AI-related techniques become part of detection engineering across information technology and operational technology environments. [01-c3] [01-c5]

And for executives, keep the limitation in view. The strongest evidence here is United States-centric and heavily electricity-sector focused. It does not prove that autonomous agents are broadly running critical infrastructure today.

What it does show is that AI governance has moved from policy debate toward control evidence. [01-c5]
