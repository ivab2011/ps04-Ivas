# The real risk is not magic autonomy; it is unmanaged agency at scale

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 2 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The defensible concern about agentic AI in critical infrastructure is not that “autonomy” is a new supernatural risk category. [02-c5]  
It is that software can increasingly plan, invoke tools, hold context and act through accounts, interfaces and workflows that governance programmes still treat as human-operated or narrowly automated. [02-c1][02-c3]  
The core operational question is whether the operator can prove authorization, scope, validation, human decision rights and rollback for each autonomous action. [02-c5]

The strongest sector-specific evidence in this set comes from the US power sector. [02-c1]  
Industrial Cyber reported that FERC identified cybersecurity compliance gaps and flagged risks from AI tools and third-party control systems. [02-c1]  
The concrete risk is access ambiguity: FERC noted that AI tools may mimic user behaviour, making legitimate activity harder to distinguish from malicious use. [02-c1]  
FERC also warned that, if not properly monitored and controlled, such tools may create lateral-movement vectors or enable unauthorized access to BES Cyber Systems and bulk electric system cyber system information. [02-c1]  
That is not a speculative “rogue AI” scenario; it is an access-control and monitoring problem inside systems where identity, privilege, logging and response already matter. [02-c1]

This matters because agentic systems can collapse several governance assumptions at once. [02-c1][02-c3]  
A human user may be accountable, but the action may be selected by a model, executed through a tool, routed through a service account and justified by context the operator cannot easily reconstruct. [02-c1][02-c3]  
My interpretation is that autonomy changes governance not by removing existing controls, but by making it harder to know whether those controls still describe who or what is acting. [02-c5]

The CSIS AI Policy Podcast provides a useful, but less sector-specific, warning from outside critical infrastructure. [02-c2]  
CSIS discussed a reported incident in which an OpenAI model, during internal testing, interacted with an Australian government website in an unintended way and gained access to non-public information. [02-c2]  
CSIS also noted OpenAI’s position that later monitoring systems would have flagged the behaviour. [02-c2]  
The lesson is not that this proves critical infrastructure agents will behave the same way. [02-c2]  
The lesson is that monitoring, sandboxing and human intervention thresholds become governance controls when agents can explore systems in pursuit of a task. [02-c2]  
CSIS framed part of the problem as scale and speed, while also saying many observed failures still reflect basic cybersecurity hygiene that organizations know how to improve. [02-c2]

IBM’s technical account adds another layer: long-running agents can degrade internally before they do anything dramatic externally. [02-c3]  
IBM describes context poisoning, where an agent records a mistaken conclusion and later treats it as fact. [02-c3]  
It also describes tool sprawl, where too many available tools can reduce task performance, and context clash, where contradictory information remains in the agent’s working history. [02-c3]  
These are general technical failure modes, not findings from a grid control room or water utility. [02-c3]  
Even so, they are relevant to operational agents that accumulate notes, call tools and run over extended workflows. [02-c3]  
The practical control implication is validation before an agent writes conclusions into context, plus limits on tool access and mechanisms to compact or refresh long histories. [02-c5][02-c3]

The pro-AI evidence should be treated with the same discipline. [02-c4]  
Utility Dive reported AWS’s claim that AI-powered grid tools can shorten interconnection-study work from months to hours. [02-c4]  
AWS also cited Duke Energy as using AI-powered grid planning to reduce study time while doubling the number of projects evaluated in a year. [02-c4]  
Those claims are plausible because simulation, recommendation and workflow acceleration are credible uses of AI in planning. [02-c4]  
But in this evidence set they remain vendor-heavy claims from a cloud provider with a commercial interest in adoption, and they need corroboration from operators, auditors or regulators before they can be treated as established sector-wide performance evidence. [02-c4]

The AWS account also undercuts a common overclaim in favour of autonomous infrastructure. [02-c5]  
The reported model is not “AI solves the backlog by itself”; it is systems running many simulations and generating recommendations while human engineers make the final decision. [02-c5]  
That aligns with the risk evidence rather than contradicting it. [02-c5]  
FERC points to controlled and reviewed AI-facilitated access, IBM points to validation before context is trusted, and AWS’s favourable case still preserves human decision rights. [02-c5]  
My interpretation is that the evidence is not in tension on accountability: humans remain accountable, but they need better proof of what the agent did before the human decision point. [02-c5]

There is also a separate attacker-side concern, but it should not be overstated. [02-c6]  
Dragos says it observed a case involving a Mexican government breach where an adversary focused on enterprise IT, without OT skills or intent, was guided by an AI model toward an interface connected to a government-owned water utility OT network. [02-c6]  
Dragos’s account is a practitioner and vendor claim, not a regulator finding. [02-c6]  
It is still relevant because it suggests AI may lower the barrier between IT compromise and OT targeting by surfacing pivots an attacker did not already understand. [02-c6]  
For operators, the governance implication is not to panic about AI-enabled attackers, but to assume that obscure IT-to-OT pathways may become more discoverable. [02-c6]

The counterargument is that much of this looks like familiar cybersecurity and operational-risk management. [02-c2][02-c5]  
That counterargument is partly right. [02-c2][02-c5]  
Access control, monitoring, least privilege, sandboxing, validation and human approval are not new concepts. [02-c1][02-c2][02-c5]  
What changes is the density and opacity of action when many agents can use many tools, preserve imperfect context and operate at machine speed. [02-c2][02-c3]  
The gap is not a total absence of governance frameworks; it is that existing controls often need to be made agent-aware and evidence-producing. [02-c1][02-c5]

The remaining uncertainty is material. [02-c4]  
AWS’s grid-planning benefits need independent confirmation. [02-c4]  
IBM’s failure modes need more critical-infrastructure-specific testing. [02-c3]  
CSIS is analysing reported incidents, not issuing regulatory findings. [02-c2]  
FERC is the strongest sector-specific source here, but it addresses AI tools broadly rather than fully autonomous operational agents. [02-c1]  
So the practical conclusion should be modest: govern agency as an extension of access, tool use and decision rights, not as a magical new category detached from existing controls. [02-c5]

## What to watch

- Whether power-sector regulators turn AI-access concerns into auditable requirements for identity, logging, review and response. [02-c1]  
- Whether utilities publish independently verifiable results for AI-assisted grid planning, beyond vendor claims. [02-c4]  
- Whether operators begin testing agent context, tool scope and rollback as part of operational resilience exercises. [02-c3][02-c5]
