# AI governance has moved from policy debate to control evidence

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 1 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The important change is not that autonomous agents are suddenly running critical infrastructure end to end. [01-c5]  

The change is that AI capabilities are being pulled into existing critical-infrastructure governance mechanisms: access control, asset categorisation, communications paths, detection logic, modelling data and accountable operators. [01-c5]  

That is narrower than “AI safety” rhetoric, but it matters more for executives because it points to evidence they can be asked to produce. [01-c5]  

FERC, NERC, MITRE and Dragos are not saying the same thing, and they are not equally authoritative for every claim. [01-c1][01-c2][01-c3][01-c4]  

But read together, they show a shift from abstract AI policy debate toward operational control evidence. [01-c5]  

FERC’s 2026 CIP audit lessons are the clearest example. [01-c1] According to the Industrial Cyber report, FERC staff highlighted access-control risks as “super intelligence” tools, including chatbots, personal assistants and automated analytical systems, become more embedded in networks supporting critical infrastructure protection assets. [01-c1] That is a reported fact about what FERC staff flagged, not proof that every bulk-power entity has deployed agentic AI in a sensitive environment. [01-c1] It is also broader than agentic AI, because the cited category includes chatbots and analytical systems as well as more autonomous tools. [01-c1]  

The governance point is concrete: if an AI-like tool touches or supports a CIP-networked environment, the question becomes who or what has access, how that access is authorised, and how activity is monitored. [01-c1] FERC’s framing matters because the CIP Reliability Standards are not described as either explicitly allowing or prohibiting these tools; instead, entities are told to consider the risks these tools pose when evaluating BES Cyber Systems and to apply appropriate controls. [01-c5] That moves AI governance into a familiar compliance pattern: determine whether the tool changes the categorisation, exposure or control requirements of a system already governed under reliability rules. [01-c5]  

For executives, AI inventory cannot sit in a separate innovation register disconnected from regulated assets. [01-c5] If an AI assistant, analytics feature or automated workflow is embedded in or adjacent to a governed environment, it may become relevant to access governance and monitoring evidence. [01-c1][01-c5]  

The second development is about AI infrastructure rather than AI decision-making. [01-c2] Utility Dive reported that NERC stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centres, crypto mining facilities and other computational loads, an important step toward finalisation. [01-c2] That is a reported fact about proposed reliability standards moving closer to adoption, not a statement that they are already fully enforceable everywhere. [01-c2]  

The significance is that large AI-related loads are being translated into grid-reliability requirements. [01-c2][01-c5] The proposed standards are described as giving transmission operators, balancing authorities and reliability coordinators the computational-load site data needed to plan, monitor and assess reliable operation of the bulk power system. [01-c5] They are also described as establishing communications capabilities with predefined protocols so operators can take prompt action to prevent or mitigate reliability impacts. [01-c5]  

This is not governance of an AI model’s reasoning process; it is governance of the physical and operational footprint that AI demand creates on the grid. [01-c2][01-c5] For boards and senior risk leaders, the control owner may not be the AI team; accountable functions may include grid planning, operations, interconnection, reliability compliance and communications procedures. [01-c5]  

The third development is that threat-modelling frameworks are starting to represent AI-assisted activity across IT and OT boundaries. [01-c3] Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE ATT&CK v20 added campaigns documenting attacks on more than 30 Minnesota water and wastewater utilities and an AI-assisted breach of Mexican government infrastructure that crossed IT and OT environments and reached a municipal water utility. [01-c3] That is a reported fact about ATT&CK coverage, not evidence that AI-assisted attacks are now common across all critical-infrastructure sectors. [01-c3]  

The same report says MITRE expanded enterprise coverage with techniques addressing prompt injection, AI-enabled data exfiltration, AI result poisoning and attacks targeting AI-assisted defences. [01-c5] The governance consequence is practical: detection engineering, adversary emulation and incident response playbooks need to include AI-assisted behaviours where they affect defended environments. [01-c3][01-c5] Prompt injection becomes a detection and testing problem; AI-enabled data exfiltration becomes a data-loss and monitoring problem; AI result poisoning becomes an integrity and assurance problem; and attacks on AI-assisted defences become a resilience and validation problem. [01-c5]  

Dragos adds a useful practitioner perspective, but it should be read differently from FERC, NERC or MITRE. [01-c4] In a September 2026 presentation, Dragos said AI in OT offence and defence had moved beyond conference hype and had become “real” for its field view over the preceding months. [01-c4] That is a source claim from an OT security vendor with commercial incentives, not independent proof of systemic prevalence. [01-c4] It is still relevant because it reflects how at least one OT security practitioner organisation is now framing AI’s role in attacker and defender workflows. [01-c4]  

The stronger governance evidence is narrower than the vendor narrative: FERC points to access control and monitoring for AI-like tools near CIP assets; NERC’s standards process points to computational-load data and communications protocols for reliability operations; and MITRE points to detection logic for AI-assisted activity crossing IT and OT boundaries. [01-c1][01-c2][01-c3][01-c5]  

My interpretation is that AI is being governed less as a standalone abstract technology category and more as a controlled actor, software feature, detection problem and grid load. [01-c5] The executive question is not only whether the organisation has an AI policy, but whether AI-enabled capabilities have been mapped to existing control evidence: authorised access, asset evaluation, communication paths, monitoring coverage, modelling assumptions and operational accountability. [01-c5]  

The limitations are important. [01-c1][01-c2][01-c3][01-c4] The strongest evidence here is US-centric and heavily weighted toward the electricity sector. [01-c1][01-c2] FERC’s item concerns audited entities and audit lessons, not the whole sector. [01-c1] NERC’s item concerns proposed standards for AI data centres and other computational loads moving toward adoption, not a completed global regime for AI infrastructure. [01-c2] MITRE’s update shows how threat modelling is evolving, but it does not by itself measure prevalence or likelihood across all operators. [01-c3] Dragos says the field has changed, but that remains a vendor/practitioner claim rather than independent sector-wide evidence. [01-c4]  

The remaining uncertainty is therefore not whether AI belongs in critical-infrastructure governance, but how quickly regulators and auditors will convert these signals into repeatable examination criteria across sectors and jurisdictions. [01-c1][01-c2][01-c3][01-c5]  

## What to watch

- Whether CIP auditors begin asking for explicit evidence of AI-like tools, access paths and monitoring controls in BES Cyber System evaluations. [01-c1][01-c5]  
- Whether NERC’s computational-load standards become enforceable requirements and how operators evidence site data, communications protocols and reliability coordination. [01-c2][01-c5]  
- Whether ATT&CK-driven detection content for prompt injection, AI-enabled exfiltration and AI-assisted OT activity becomes part of routine security validation. [01-c3][01-c5]
