# What operators should do next: make AI governable before it becomes operationally normal

*As of 2026-10-18. Part 3 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

Critical-infrastructure operators should not wait for AI-specific law to become tidy before making AI governable in operational environments. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c3] [03-c4] [03-c6]  
The practical move is to translate agentic AI into objects existing GRC processes can see: assets, accounts, authorizations, data reach, external communications, vendor dependencies, failure modes and community impacts. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4] [03-c5] [03-c6]

Ampyx Cyber’s source claim, reported in Industrial Cyber, is the most direct operational starting point: entities should know which products in their CIP-networked environment have AI features, including features a vendor enabled after purchase. [03-c1]  
That turns “AI adoption” into an inventory problem: can the operator identify AI functionality already present in regulated environments, including functionality added through a vendor update rather than a new procurement? [03-c1]

Ampyx’s second source claim makes the evidence file more concrete: for each AI tool, an entity should be able to show the CIP-004 authorization, the account it runs under, the BCSI it can reach and the communications it makes outside the ESP. [03-c2]  
If the tool has an account, reaches protected information or communicates outside the security perimeter, it belongs in the compliance and security evidence base. [03-c2]

My interpretation is that this is the near-term operating model for agentic AI in critical infrastructure: prove what the system can reach, what it can do, who authorized it and where it talks. [03-c1] [03-c2]  
That gives procurement teams sharper vendor questions: disclose AI features already present, features that can be enabled later and the accounts, data paths and external communications those features require. [03-c1] [03-c2]  
This is not a call for a new AI bureaucracy; it is a call to make AI inspectable by the governance machinery operators already use. [03-c1] [03-c2]

The same logic applies to technical testing. [03-c6]  
IBM, a vendor source with a commercial incentive, argues in a recent video that agent failures can be reduced by deliberately limiting what the agent retains or can use: validate conclusions against something real, compact long histories, curate tools and prune conflicting state. [03-c6]  
Because this is vendor educational content rather than independent operational evidence, it should not be treated as proof that these controls are sufficient in critical infrastructure. [03-c6]  
It is still a useful engineering checklist for tests that security and OT teams can run before an agent is allowed near operational workflows. [03-c6]

The deeper point is that “more context” and “more tools” are not automatically safer for an agent. [03-c6]  
IBM’s source claim is that the fixes for several agent failure modes work by throwing something away: unsupported conclusions, excessive history, unnecessary tools or superseded state. [03-c6]  
For operators, that suggests a control design principle: restrict agent visibility and tool access to the job, then test whether the agent fails safely when context is incomplete, stale or contradictory. [03-c6]

The governance perimeter also extends beyond software running inside the operator’s environment. [03-c3] [03-c4]  
Utility Dive reports as a fact that proposed NERC reliability standards for computational loads would apply to loads of at least 50 MW connected at voltage levels of at least 100 kV. [03-c3]  
Utility Dive also reports that NERC’s board is expected to consider the new standards in the fourth quarter, based on the development timelines in the proposals. [03-c3]  
Executives should watch whether these standards are approved and how their scope affects AI data-center interconnections. [03-c3]

For AI data centers, governance is not only a grid-modeling problem. [03-c4]  
On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun argued from her reporting that industry actors can over-extrapolate from narrow corrections about water use and wrongly dismiss the wider backlash as misinformation. [03-c4]  
She identified other concerns, including nuisance, electricity use, affordability impacts, opaque deals and a sense that local communities are not getting a fair bargain. [03-c4]  
My interpretation is that operators, data-center customers and their advisers should treat local trust, electricity impacts and deal transparency as governance risks, not public-relations afterthoughts. [03-c4]

There is a useful precedent outside AI, but it must be handled carefully. [03-c5]  
Industrial Cyber reports that Singapore’s quantum-safe guidance recommends that exposed organizations put migration plans in place by 2027 and aim to complete migration by 2035. [03-c5]  
The same report says the guidance includes vendor and partner engagement because an organization’s readiness depends on theirs, and that pilot participants must submit reports of learnings and demonstrated impact. [03-c5]  
As an analogy only, this shows how regulators can turn an emerging technology risk into exposure assessment, plans, vendor engagement, deadlines and pilot evidence. [03-c5]  
It does not show that AI governance will follow the same schedule, legal route or technical pattern. [03-c5]

The main limitation is the evidence base.  
The Singapore example is a governance precedent, not evidence about AI systems. [03-c5]  
There is also little direct operator testimony from EU critical-infrastructure entities in the accepted evidence.  
Community and worker perspectives appear mainly through one CSIS podcast discussion of US data centers, so they should be treated as important but incomplete evidence. [03-c4]

The remaining uncertainty is not whether AI will create a completely new governance universe. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c6]  
The uncertainty is how quickly existing audit, reliability, procurement and siting processes will be updated to make AI-specific evidence mandatory. [03-c2] [03-c3] [03-c4] [03-c5]  
Operators do not need to know that answer before acting. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c6]  
They can start by making every AI feature visible, accountable, bounded and testable. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c6]

## What to watch

- Whether NERC’s computational-load standards are approved, and how the 50 MW and 100 kV thresholds affect AI data-center interconnections. [03-c3]  
- Whether vendors can disclose AI features enabled after procurement, plus the accounts, protected information reach and external communications those features require. [03-c1] [03-c2]  
- Whether agent testing moves from demonstrations to evidence that validation, compaction, tool curation and pruning reduce failure modes in operational settings. [03-c6]
