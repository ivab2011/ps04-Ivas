# What operators should do next: make agent governance auditable

*As of 2026-10-19. Part 3 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The practical lesson from this week’s evidence is simple: operators should treat agentic AI governance as an auditable readiness program, not as a research topic waiting for future rules. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4] [03-c5]

The near-term work is to build evidence files showing what AI-enabled tools exist, who owns them, what accounts they use, what systems and data they can reach, what communications they make, what vendor obligations apply, and how controls have been tested. [03-c1] [03-c4]

That is not because every AI tool is already an autonomous operational agent; it is because critical-infrastructure governance is moving toward scope definition, accountable ownership, asset registers, risk assessment, control testing, documentation and milestones. [03-c2] [03-c4] [03-c5]

For U.S. bulk-power entities, the clearest immediate signal comes from Industrial Cyber’s reporting on FERC cybersecurity compliance gaps and Ampyx Cyber’s analysis of the 2026 CIP audit findings. [03-c1]

Ampyx says entities should be able to show, for each AI tool, the CIP-004 authorization, the account it runs under, the Bulk Electric System Cyber System Information it can reach, and the communications it makes outside the Electronic Security Perimeter. [03-c1]

Industrial Cyber separately reported FERC’s concern that entities should first consider the business need for system integrity tools in CIP-networked environments and should properly grant, regularly verify and timely revoke electronic access to BES Cyber Systems and BCSI facilitated by those tools. [03-c1]

The governance implication is not “ban AI from operational environments,” but “make the access path, identity, purpose and communication pattern reviewable by an auditor.” [03-c1]

That matters for agentic AI because autonomy often changes the control question from “who clicked the button?” to “which account, policy and toolchain allowed this action?” [03-c1]

If an AI-enabled product can query sensitive information, initiate workflows, recommend control actions or communicate outside a protected zone, the evidence file should show why that access exists and how it is bounded. [03-c1]

The second lesson is to tier AI uses by consequence rather than by novelty. [03-c2]

A sponsored nuclear-sector article in Industry Dive argues that knowledge tools, predictive tools and operational optimization tools impose rising requirements for data, integration, validation and governance as AI moves closer to plant decisions. [03-c2]

Because that article is sponsored content, its claims should be treated as commercial source claims, not neutral regulatory evidence. [03-c2] [03-c3]

Even so, the principle is useful: a document-search assistant, a predictive maintenance model and an optimization tool near operational decisions should not all receive the same validation, human-review and change-control treatment. [03-c2]

For operators, the AI inventory should classify uses by operational proximity, potential failure impact, required human review, validation evidence and rollback options. [03-c2] [03-c3]

The third lesson is to measure AI programs by operating value and risk reduction, not adoption volume. [03-c3]

The sponsored Industry Dive article claims that roughly 60% of AI initiatives it observes are in active use without benefits being effectively quantified. [03-c3]

It also argues that priority applications should be tied to operating measures such as engineering search time or outage planning cycle time, rather than licenses or usage counts. [03-c3]

For a board or executive risk committee, the stronger question is not “how many AI tools have we deployed?” but “which operational risk, delay or control weakness did this tool reduce, and what evidence shows that?” [03-c3]

The fourth lesson comes from outside the U.S. and EU, and should be used carefully. [03-c4]

Industrial Cyber reported Nozomi’s summary of India’s power-sector cybersecurity framework, under which regulations require visibility, monitoring, governance, asset management, risk assessment, incident response and supply-chain controls across IT and OT environments. [03-c4]

This is vendor commentary on a non-U.S./EU power-sector framework, so it is not evidence of what U.S. or European AI-agent rules require today. [03-c4]

Still, it shows a familiar compliance trajectory: broad cyber-risk language becomes explicit obligations around leadership, asset registers, monitoring, incident response and vendor controls. [03-c4]

Singapore’s quantum-safe guidance provides a second analogy, not AI evidence. [03-c5]

Industrial Cyber reported that Singapore’s Cyber Security Agency recommended that organizations exposed to quantum-related cybersecurity risks establish migration plans by 2027 and aim to complete transition by 2035. [03-c5]

The same reporting says Critical Information Infrastructure systems are called on to complete migration by 2031. [03-c5]

The point is not that quantum migration tells us how AI agents fail; it is that regulators can turn an emerging technical risk into a dated critical-infrastructure program with exposure assessment, ownership, vendor engagement and completion milestones. [03-c5]

AI operators should prepare similar evidence now: inventories, tiering decisions, vendor attestations, access reviews, validation records, monitoring logs and board-level milestones. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4] [03-c5]

There is also an infrastructure angle that boards should not treat as separate from AI governance. [03-c6] [03-c7]

Utility Dive reported that multiple NERC Project 2026-02 standards for computational loads include requirements around interconnection studies and modeling, operational data and communications, load protection coordination and disturbance monitoring. [03-c6]

It also reported that NERC’s board is expected to consider the standards in the fourth quarter, based on the proposals’ development timelines. [03-c6]

For AI data centers and other large computational loads, operators should watch whether NERC and FERC actions convert today’s modeling, data-sharing and disturbance-monitoring expectations into reliability requirements. [03-c6]

CSIS’s AI policy podcast adds a public-acceptance risk that executives should put on the same register. [03-c7]

In that discussion, Jasmine Sun described local opposition driven by neighborhood disruption, electricity demand, possible emissions, construction impacts, potential rate effects and distrust of negotiations conducted under nondisclosure agreements. [03-c7]

Data-center governance should include public acceptance, local-deal transparency and energy-impact communication, not only grid interconnection and physical security. [03-c7]

The limitation is that this evidence does not yet amount to a binding U.S. or EU agentic-AI rulebook for critical infrastructure.

Singapore’s example is a quantum-safe migration precedent, India’s framework is outside the U.S. and EU and summarized through vendor commentary, and the nuclear operating-value article is sponsored content. [03-c2] [03-c3] [03-c4] [03-c5]

What remains uncertain is whether U.S. and European regulators will create AI-specific critical-infrastructure duties, adapt existing cybersecurity rules, or continue relying mainly on voluntary AI governance frameworks.

But waiting for that answer is the wrong operating posture. [03-c1] [03-c4] [03-c5]

The direction of travel is already visible: regulators and standards bodies ask operators to define scope, identify assets, assign owners, test controls, manage vendors, document decisions and meet milestones. [03-c4] [03-c5] [03-c6]

For agentic AI, the readiness question is practical: if an auditor asked tomorrow why an AI-enabled tool has a credential, what it can reach, what it can send and how its output is reviewed, could the operator produce the file? [03-c1] [03-c2]

## What to watch

- Whether NERC’s computational-load standards move from proposal to adopted reliability requirements covering modeling, operational data, communications and disturbance monitoring. [03-c6]

- Whether U.S. and EU critical-infrastructure regulators translate AI recommendations into auditable obligations around inventory, access, validation, vendor controls and evidence retention. [03-c1] [03-c2] [03-c4]

- Whether data-center approvals face stronger demands for transparency around electricity impacts, emissions, construction disruption and local negotiations. [03-c7]
