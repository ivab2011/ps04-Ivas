# AI governance became operational, not theoretical

*As of 2026-10-19. Part 1 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The important change is not that a new AI model can do something dramatic. It is that AI risk is being translated into operational governance questions: who or what has access, which assets are in scope, how computational demand behaves on the grid, and whether affected communities trust the infrastructure decisions being made around AI. [01-c1][01-c3][01-c4][01-c6]

For critical-infrastructure executives, this moves agentic AI governance out of the policy-paper category and into cyber access control, vendor management, reliability planning and permitting risk. [01-c6]

The clearest signal comes from the US electricity sector. Industrial Cyber reported that FERC’s 2026 CIP audit lessons addressed AI-enabled “SI tools” under existing Critical Infrastructure Protection access-control expectations for BES Cyber Systems and BES Cyber System Information. [01-c1]

That is a reported fact about audit lessons, not a new AI-specific CIP standard. [01-c1] The practical point is still important: FERC’s lesson treats AI-enabled tools as objects that may receive, process or facilitate access to regulated cyber systems and information. [01-c1] That makes AI governance an access-control problem, not only a model-risk problem. [01-c1]

The same report said entities told auditors that vendors and suppliers are adding SI tools to products and services while compliance programs are slow to adapt. [01-c2] Many operators will not first encounter AI through an internal AI strategy, but through upgraded vendor platforms, managed services, monitoring tools and product features already inside operational or compliance boundaries. [01-c2]

For boards and CISOs, the control question is therefore not “do we use AI?” but “where has AI already entered our regulated environment, under whose authority, and with what access?” [01-c1][01-c2]

The second signal is load behavior. Utility Dive reported that NERC stakeholders approved new reliability standards for AI data centers, crypto miners and other computational loads, moving those standards closer to final adoption. [01-c3] Utility Dive also reported NERC’s stated aim: requirements to support reliable integration of large computational loads into the bulk power system. [01-c3]

This is not about governing an AI model inside a control room. It is about the physical infrastructure footprint of AI and other computational activity becoming material to bulk-power reliability. [01-c3]

My interpretation is that this widens the governance perimeter for AI infrastructure. [01-c6] If large computational loads reshape demand profiles and expose reliability gaps across operational and planning horizons, then AI governance for infrastructure operators cannot stop at cybersecurity questionnaires or acceptable-use policies. [01-c6] It has to include grid connection assumptions, load modeling, curtailment behavior, emergency coordination and the allocation of reliability obligations among operators, customers and service providers. [01-c3][01-c6]

The third signal is legitimacy. In a CSIS AI Policy Podcast discussion, Jasmine Sun argued that local opposition to data centers often has less to do with AI itself than with infrastructure effects, electricity prices, emissions, pollution, quality of life and distrust in the decision-making process. [01-c4] She also described concern about projects negotiated under non-disclosure arrangements and perceived “backroom” processes, which communities may experience as unfair even before they debate the technology’s merits. [01-c4]

That is a source claim from a policy podcast, not a regulator finding. [01-c4] Still, it adds a governance dimension that cyber and reliability frameworks often handle only indirectly. [01-c4][01-c6] Public acceptance of AI infrastructure can fail because people believe they are bearing local costs while outside companies control the information, legal leverage and economic upside. [01-c4]

For executives, permitting and community engagement are not peripheral communications tasks. [01-c4][01-c6] They are part of the governance system for AI infrastructure, especially where data centers change energy demand, land use, emissions profiles or local trust. [01-c4][01-c6]

A fourth signal comes from cyber operations. Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE ATT&CK v20 expanded coverage of critical-infrastructure threats, AI-enabled attacks and cyber espionage. [01-c5] The same report said the Enterprise matrix added techniques and sub-techniques covering prompt injection, AI-enabled exfiltration, AI result poisoning, attacks on AI-assisted defenses, CAPTCHA evasion and application-specific password theft. [01-c5]

That matters because some AI-related cyber risks are being converted from broad narratives into named behaviors that defenders can map, monitor and test against. [01-c5] This does not prove that every AI attack scenario is equally likely or equally relevant to OT environments. [01-c5] It does show that the security community is beginning to express parts of the AI risk surface in the operational language of techniques and detections. [01-c5]

Taken together, these developments point to a shift in what “AI governance” means for critical infrastructure. [01-c6] The relevant unit is no longer only the AI model, the chatbot, or the internal policy document. [01-c6] It is the interface between AI-enabled tools and regulated cyber environments, between computational loads and grid reliability, and between AI infrastructure buildout and public legitimacy. [01-c6] That is my inference from the FERC audit lessons, the NERC standards development reporting and the CSIS discussion of data-center backlash. [01-c6]

The limitation is that the evidence is still uneven. The strongest material here comes from the US electricity sector and AI data centers, not from European operators, water, transport or telecom. [01-c1][01-c3][01-c4] The FERC evidence concerns audit lessons and recommendations under existing CIP expectations, not a newly enforceable AI-specific regime. [01-c1] The NERC development concerns reliability standards moving toward adoption, not a settled answer to how every AI-related load will be governed in practice. [01-c3] The CSIS material is interpretive discussion based on engagement with affected communities, not a formal permitting rule or infrastructure standard. [01-c4] There is also remaining uncertainty about how quickly voluntary AI recommendations, audit lessons and defender mappings will become enforceable obligations. [01-c1][01-c5]

But waiting for a single comprehensive agentic AI rule would be a mistake. [01-c6] The governance work is already being distributed across existing regimes: CIP access control, vendor oversight, reliability standards, cyber detection frameworks and local infrastructure approval processes. [01-c1][01-c2][01-c3][01-c4][01-c5][01-c6]

For executives, the practical response is to manage agentic AI as an operating discipline before it arrives as a consolidated compliance checklist. [01-c6]

## What to watch

- Whether FERC and NERC turn AI-related audit lessons and computational-load standards into more explicit, enforceable obligations for registered entities. [01-c1][01-c3]

- Whether vendors can document where AI-enabled tools touch BES Cyber Systems, BES Cyber System Information or adjacent operational environments. [01-c1][01-c2]

- Whether data-center developers and operators can address reliability impacts and community legitimacy before opposition hardens into permitting risk. [01-c3][01-c4]
