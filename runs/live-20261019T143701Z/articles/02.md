# The evidence supports containment risk, not sci-fi autonomy claims

*As of 2026-10-19. Part 2 of 3 in the Agentic GRC series.*

The best-supported risk is not that autonomous AI agents can independently decide to take down critical infrastructure.[02-c1] The stronger case is narrower and more operational: AI can compress exploit reproduction, mishandle tools and context, and generate activity at a scale that strains human review.[02-c2][02-c4][02-c5]

That distinction matters for governance.[02-c7] If the failure mode is unconstrained access rather than autonomous intent, the priority is containment: sandboxing, scoped credentials, tool limits, network segmentation, validation, detection and incident handling outside the model.[02-c7] Operators should treat model instructions as useful but insufficient, because the evidence points to failures in tool use, context selection and monitoring capacity rather than to reliable self-restraint by agents.[02-c5][02-c7]

Claroty Team82 provides the clearest OT-specific example in the evidence set.[02-c1] According to the Industrial Cyber report, researchers used Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 4.6 through Cursor to reproduce Danfoss controller vulnerabilities and chain two of them to obtain root-level command execution on a lab device.[02-c1] That is serious, but it is not proof of autonomous zero-day discovery.[02-c1] The report says the experiment relied on public vulnerability disclosures and human intervention to correct the AI agent’s initial errors.[02-c1]

The governance implication is still material.[02-c2] Claroty’s reported work took about 28 minutes from initial analysis to a working exploit and cost roughly $6 in AI usage.[02-c2] For internet-facing OT assets, the concern is lower cost, lower time and potentially lower skill thresholds for reproducing known weaknesses.[02-c2] The report also notes that internet-facing systems may be reachable without first breaching an internal network, making exposure management part of the AI-risk discussion rather than a separate OT hygiene issue.[02-c2]

MITRE’s ATT&CK update points in the same practical direction.[02-c3] Industrial Cyber reports that ATT&CK v20 added campaigns covering attacks on more than 30 Minnesota water and wastewater utilities and an AI-assisted breach of Mexican government infrastructure that crossed IT and OT environments and reached a municipal water utility.[02-c3] The update also expanded enterprise coverage for prompt injection, AI-enabled data exfiltration, AI result poisoning and attacks targeting AI-assisted defenses.[02-c3] That does not prove frequent AI-enabled OT incidents, but it shows the behaviors are concrete enough to become defender taxonomies and detections.[02-c3]

The CSIS podcast adds a less directly evidenced concern: scale.[02-c4] CSIS discussed reports that OpenAI and Anthropic were investigating tens of thousands of problematic frontier-model incidents, and it cited Sam Altman’s statement about petabytes of agent activity logs.[02-c4] Its interpretation was not that every incident is catastrophic, but that monitoring and analysis capacity may not match the scale and speed of deployed agents.[02-c4] For operators considering externally connected agents, oversight must be designed in if activity is too large or fast to review.[02-c4]

There is also evidence that agents can become less reliable when given too much information or too many tools.[02-c5] IBM’s context-engineering discussion, which should be treated as a vendor source claim, cites research in which frontier models became less reliable as context windows grew.[02-c5] IBM also describes a benchmark in which a model failed a task when offered 46 tools but passed the same task when offered 19 tools.[02-c5] Its recommended fixes are to compact, curate and prune context and tools, supporting a governance requirement to validate what agents can see and use rather than assuming more context improves performance.[02-c5]

Dragos offers a practitioner account that fits the same containment logic, though it is also a commercial OT security source and some details are not public.[02-c6] Dragos says that, in a Mexican government breach, the adversary focused on enterprise IT and lacked OT skills, knowledge and intent.[02-c6] Dragos further says the AI model highlighted an interface to a government-owned water utility’s OT networks and steered attention toward OT as a high-value target.[02-c6] If accurate, the risk is not only AI-written malware; it is AI guidance that helps attackers identify OT paths they may not otherwise understand.[02-c6]

Taken together, these sources support a hard-control model of agent governance.[02-c7] Industrial Cyber reports a view from Maor that the answer is “hard guardrails,” meaning technical restrictions enforced outside the model.[02-c7] Claroty’s experiment required human expertise to recognize incorrect results, ask the right questions and validate the outcome, reinforcing validation rather than blind automation.[02-c7] IBM’s tool-curation argument points in the same direction: give the agent only the tools the task calls for, and fetch more only when needed.[02-c7]

The limitation is that much of this evidence is not incident-rate evidence.[02-c4][02-c6] Claroty’s case is a lab reproduction of disclosed vulnerabilities, not autonomous discovery from scratch.[02-c1] CSIS is interpreting reports about AI-lab and government-website incidents, but the accepted evidence here does not include the original incident reports.[02-c4] Dragos is describing field experience as a commercial practitioner, with some details outside the public record.[02-c6] IBM’s analysis is useful for agent design, but it is not OT incident evidence.[02-c5]

The defensible conclusion is restrained but actionable.[02-c7] The evidence does not justify broad claims that agents are independently capable of taking down critical infrastructure.[02-c1] It does justify treating agents as systems that can accelerate known attack paths, mishandle excessive context and tools, and create monitoring burdens that exceed human review capacity.[02-c2][02-c4][02-c5] For critical-infrastructure operators, the governance gap is whether the organization can constrain and verify what the model, its tools and its credentials can do.[02-c7]

## What to watch

- Whether future OT research moves from reproducing disclosed vulnerabilities to credible evidence of autonomous vulnerability discovery.[02-c1]
- Whether AI-agent monitoring practices can keep pace with the scale of activity described by CSIS.[02-c4]
- Whether operator controls shift from prompt-level rules to enforceable limits on tools, credentials, network reachability and context.[02-c7]
