# AI governance became operational, not theoretical

[VISUAL: Title slide — “From AI policy to operational governance” — Agentic GRC]

What changed when artificial intelligence moved from boardroom debate into critical infrastructure operations?

[VISUAL: Slide — “1. AI tools are now access-control objects” — Source: Industrial Cyber, on FERC audit lessons]

The first change is cyber governance.

Industrial Cyber reported on Federal Energy Regulatory Commission audit lessons for the United States power sector. FERC is the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission.

Those audit lessons put artificial intelligence enabled tools inside the existing access-control problem for Bulk Electric System Cyber Systems, and for Bulk Electric System Cyber System Information. [01-c1]

In plain English, AI tools are not only software features. They can become paths into regulated systems and regulated information.

The audit lessons refer to Critical Infrastructure Protection requirements, known as CIP, and say access by these tools should be authorized. [01-c1]

That makes the governance question practical.

Who approved the tool?

What systems can it reach?

What information can it process?

And who reviews access when the vendor changes the product?

Industrial Cyber also reported that audited entities said many vendors and suppliers are adding these tools to products and services while compliance programs are slower to adapt. [01-c2]

So the risk is not only a future autonomous agent making a dramatic decision.

It is also a vendor feature arriving before access-control, review, and supply-chain processes have caught up. [01-c2]

[VISUAL: Slide — “2. AI demand is becoming a reliability issue” — Source: Utility Dive, on NERC standards]

The second change is grid reliability.

Utility Dive reported that North American Electric Reliability Corporation stakeholders approved new reliability standards for artificial intelligence data centers, crypto mining facilities, and other large computational loads. [01-c3]

NERC said the standards are intended to support reliable integration of large computational loads into the bulk power system. [01-c3]

That framing matters.

Artificial intelligence infrastructure is not only a customer demand forecast.

It is becoming a power-system behavior that planners and operators may need to model, manage, and eventually hold to reliability obligations. [01-c3]

My interpretation is that this moves AI governance beyond model risk and cyber risk.

For electricity operators, it also includes load behavior, planning horizons, and the operational consequences of very large computational facilities. [01-c6]

[VISUAL: Slide — “3. Community trust is part of infrastructure governance” — Source: CSIS AI Policy Podcast]

The third change is public legitimacy.

On the CSIS AI Policy Podcast, Jasmine Sun said that when she spoke with people about data centers, artificial intelligence itself rarely came up. [01-c4]

Her claim was that concerns were mostly about infrastructure effects, quality of life, distrust of corporations, and the way local deals were made. [01-c4]

She also described worries about emissions, electricity prices, behind-the-meter gas plants in Texas, and local processes that felt undemocratic. [01-c4]

For executives, this is still governance.

If communities believe they are being asked to absorb power, land, emissions, cost, or quality-of-life impacts without a fair process, data-center buildout becomes harder to permit and harder to defend. [01-c4]

My interpretation is that AI infrastructure governance now includes explaining infrastructure impacts, not only explaining models. [01-c6]

[VISUAL: Slide — “4. Some AI cyber risks are becoming observable techniques” — Source: Industrial Cyber, on MITRE ATT&CK v20]

There is also a defender-facing change.

Industrial Cyber reported that MITRE released version 20 of ATT&CK, which stands for Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge. [01-c5]

The update expands coverage of critical infrastructure threats, artificial intelligence enabled attacks, and cyber espionage. [01-c5]

For enterprise environments, it includes techniques and sub-techniques covering prompt injection, artificial intelligence enabled exfiltration, artificial intelligence result poisoning, attacks on artificial intelligence assisted defenses, CAPTCHA evasion, and application-specific password theft. [01-c5]

This does not prove that every AI attack scenario is equally likely.

But it does show that some AI-related cyber risks are being translated into defender mappings, techniques, and detections. [01-c5]

[VISUAL: Closing slide — “What to watch” — “Access, load, legitimacy”]

What should leaders watch next?

First, whether voluntary audit lessons on AI-enabled tools become enforceable requirements inside Critical Infrastructure Protection programs.

Second, how quickly NERC’s computational-load standards move from stakeholder approval toward final adoption.

Third, whether data-center developers and operators treat local trust as a governance requirement, rather than a communications problem.

The core point is simple.

Agentic AI governance is becoming operational.

It now spans cyber access, vendor management, grid reliability, and public legitimacy. [01-c6]

The evidence is strongest in the United States electricity sector and data centers.

It is weaker for Europe, water, transport, and telecom, and it remains uncertain how fast today’s recommendations become tomorrow’s obligations.
