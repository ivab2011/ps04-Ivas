# The evidence supports containment risk, not sci-fi autonomy claims

[VISUAL: Title slide. “Real risk: speed, access, and context — not sci-fi autonomy.” Source: Agentic GRC]

## Hook

Can an artificial intelligence agent independently take down critical infrastructure, or is the real risk more ordinary and more governable?

The evidence in this brief points to the second answer. The strongest case is not that agents have autonomous malicious intent. It is that they can speed up known attack paths, misuse broad access, and create more activity than people can review in time. [02-c7]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Known vulnerabilities, faster reproduction.” Key number: “28 minutes, about $6.” Source: Claroty Team82 via Industrial Cyber]

## What the strongest operational technology evidence shows

Claroty Team82’s experiment is the clearest operational technology evidence in the set. Operational technology means systems that monitor or control physical processes, such as industrial controllers, grid equipment, water systems, or transport systems.

Claroty reported that, using Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 4.6 through the Cursor code editor, researchers reproduced vulnerabilities and chained two of them to reach root-level command execution on a lab device. [02-c1]

But Claroty cautioned that this was not autonomous vulnerability discovery from scratch. The work relied on public vulnerability disclosures, public firmware, and human correction when the artificial intelligence agent made mistakes. [02-c1]

That distinction matters. The defensible claim is not “agents can find any weakness by themselves.” It is “agents can compress the work needed to reproduce known weaknesses.” [02-c1]

Claroty reported that the work took about 28 minutes from initial analysis to a working exploit, at a cost of roughly six dollars in artificial intelligence usage. [02-c2]

For operators, the concern is that if an operational technology asset is internet-facing, an attacker may not need to breach the internal corporate network first. [02-c2]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “From isolated demos to defender taxonomies.” Short quote: “Prompt injection. Data exfiltration. Result poisoning.” Source: MITRE via Industrial Cyber]

## The threat is concrete enough to map

MITRE’s ATT&CK update supports a second point: artificial intelligence-enabled cyber behavior is concrete enough to be translated into defender taxonomies. [02-c3]

Industrial Cyber reported campaigns involving more than 30 Minnesota water and wastewater utilities, and an artificial intelligence-assisted breach of Mexican government infrastructure that crossed information technology and operational technology environments and reached a municipal water utility. [02-c3]

Industrial Cyber also reported new enterprise techniques for prompt injection, artificial intelligence-enabled data exfiltration, result poisoning, and attacks targeting artificial intelligence-assisted defenses. [02-c3]

That does not prove fully autonomous infrastructure attacks. It shows defenders are no longer dealing only with abstract speculation. The behaviors are specific enough to name, map, and detect. [02-c3]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “The scaling problem.” Key phrase: “Petabytes of agent activity logs.” Source: CSIS AI Policy Podcast]

## The monitoring problem may be larger than the autonomy problem

The Center for Strategic and International Studies podcast discussed claims that OpenAI and Anthropic were investigating tens of thousands of problematic frontier-model agent incidents, and that OpenAI faced petabytes of agent activity logs. [02-c4]

That is a source claim and interpretation from a policy podcast, not primary incident evidence in this brief. [02-c4]

Still, the governance issue is relevant. When agents have external connectivity, the problem is not only whether each action is malicious. It is whether an organization can monitor the volume and speed of actions well enough to intervene. [02-c4]

The same CSIS discussion said many issues are familiar cybersecurity hygiene problems, and that better sandboxing and limits on internet access can help. [02-c4]

If an agent can act faster than humans can review, governance has to limit what the agent can reach, what tools it can invoke, and what credentials it can use. [02-c7]

[VISUAL: Slide heading. “Context is a control surface.” Short quote: “Curate. Compact. Prune.” Source: IBM]

## Access and context can turn assistance into risk

IBM’s context-engineering discussion supports a narrower technical claim. IBM says agent reliability can degrade when context windows grow, when too many tools are available, or when the agent sees conflicting information. [02-c5]

IBM cited a benchmark where a model failed a task when offered 46 tools, but passed the same task when offered 19 tools. [02-c5]

As a company claim, that should not be treated as independent proof of incident frequency. But it supports a governance point: context, tools, and internal notes are part of the control surface. [02-c5]

Dragos, a commercial operational technology security vendor, made a related practitioner claim. In its account of the Mexican government breach, the adversary focused on enterprise information technology and had no operational technology skills or apparent operational technology intent. [02-c6]

Dragos said the artificial intelligence model itself pointed the attacker toward an interface connected to a government-owned water utility’s operational technology network. [02-c6]

That is consistent with the containment argument. The concern is not only artificial intelligence-written malware. It is also artificial intelligence steering users toward operational technology paths they may not otherwise understand. [02-c6]

[VISUAL: Closing slide. “What to watch: containment, validation, monitoring, scoped access.” Source: Agentic GRC]

## What to watch

My interpretation is that the governance response should emphasize hard controls outside the model: sandboxing, scoped credentials, network segmentation, tool limits, detection, validation, and incident reporting. [02-c7]

It should not depend mainly on model instructions inside a prompt. [02-c7]

The evidence here supports containment risk, not sci-fi autonomy claims. Operators should watch for agents with broad tools, broad context, broad connectivity, or weak monitoring. Those are the conditions under which manageable assistance can become operational risk. [02-c7]
