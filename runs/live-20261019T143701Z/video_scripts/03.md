# What operators should do next: make agent governance auditable

[VISUAL: Title slide. “Agentic AI governance: make it auditable.” Source: Agentic GRC.]

What should critical-infrastructure operators do now, before autonomous AI agents become another audit finding?

The short answer is this.

Treat agentic AI governance as an auditable readiness program.

Not as a research topic, a pilot portfolio, or something owned only by innovation teams.

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “1. Start with the inventory.” Quote: “For each AI tool…” Source: Industrial Cyber / Ampyx Cyber.]

For U.S. bulk-power entities, the clearest near-term signal comes from Industrial Cyber’s reporting on Federal Energy Regulatory Commission findings, and from Ampyx Cyber’s analysis of those findings.

The practical task is to make every AI tool in Critical Infrastructure Protection, or CIP, networked environments auditable. [03-c1]

That means showing who authorized the tool under CIP-004, which account it runs under, what bulk electric system cyber system information it can reach, and what communications it makes outside the electronic security perimeter. [03-c1]

This is company analysis, not a new AI-specific rule.

But it points to the evidence file operators will need: access, identity, data reach, and external communications.

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “2. Govern by consequence.” Quote: “Match governance to consequence.” Source: Utility Dive.]

A sponsored Utility Dive article on nuclear operations makes a broader governance claim: AI uses should be governed by consequence, not novelty. [03-c2]

A knowledge tool, a predictive tool, and an operational optimization tool should not face the same validation and human-review requirements. [03-c2]

The closer a system gets to plant decisions, the stronger the requirements should become for data, integration, validation, and governance. [03-c2]

That is a commercial source claim.

But it is consistent with the control logic operators already know.

Criticality drives assurance.

The same article argues that AI programs should be measured by operating value and risk reduction, not just by how many tools are deployed or licenses used. [03-c3]

It says roughly 60 percent of active AI use is not having benefits effectively quantified, and recommends tying priority applications to measures such as engineering search time or outage planning cycle time. [03-c3]

For leaders, this means agent governance should connect to operational outcomes.

If a tool changes decisions, it needs evidence.

If it claims benefits, it needs measurement.

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “3. Readiness looks familiar.” Short text: “Scope. Owners. Assets. Risk. Controls. Evidence.” Source: Industrial Cyber / Nozomi.]

The readiness model is already visible outside the United States and Europe.

Industrial Cyber, summarizing Nozomi Networks’ view of India’s power-sector cybersecurity framework, says the regulations require visibility, monitoring, governance, asset management, risk assessment, incident response, and supply-chain controls across information technology and operational technology environments. [03-c4]

This is vendor commentary on a non-U.S. and non-European framework.

It is not an AI-agent rule.

But the pattern matters: define scope, assign accountable owners, identify and classify critical systems, monitor them, prepare incident response, control vendors, and keep records that prove it was done. [03-c4]

That same pattern should shape AI readiness now.

Operators should inventory AI-enabled products, map credentials, document external communications, classify uses by consequence, and record control evidence before sector rules become more explicit.

[VISUAL: Slide heading: “4. Emerging risks become deadlines.” Key dates: “2027, 2031, 2035.” Source: Industrial Cyber / Singapore CSA.]

Here is my interpretation, using a non-AI analogy.

Singapore’s quantum-safe guidance shows how an emerging technical risk can become a dated migration program for critical infrastructure. [03-c5]

Industrial Cyber reports that Singapore’s Cyber Security Agency recommends migration plans by 2027, completion for critical information infrastructure by 2031, and broader transition by 2035. [03-c5]

That is not evidence about AI systems.

It is evidence about how regulators can turn uncertainty into exposure assessment, ownership, vendor engagement, and milestones. [03-c5]

The lesson for AI is not that the same dates will apply.

The lesson is that voluntary preparation can quickly become auditable obligation.

[VISUAL: Closing slide. Heading: “What to watch next.” Short text: “Standards become scope.” Sources: Utility Dive / CSIS.]

What to watch next is the conversion of guidance into enforceable scope.

Utility Dive reports that North American Electric Reliability Corporation, or NERC, standards for large computational loads include requirements around interconnection studies and modeling, operational data and communications, load protection coordination, and disturbance monitoring. [03-c6]

NERC’s board was expected to consider those standards in the fourth quarter, based on proposal timelines. [03-c6]

For AI data centers and other large computational loads, that is the governance edge to watch. [03-c6]

My interpretation from the CSIS AI Policy Podcast is that boards should also add local acceptance and deal transparency to the AI infrastructure risk register. [03-c7]

The podcast reported concerns about electricity costs, emissions, construction impacts, and distrust of negotiations held under nondisclosure agreements. [03-c7]

The conclusion is practical.

Build the evidence file now.

When AI-specific controls arrive, the operators that can already show scope, ownership, access, validation, vendor duties, and test results will be in a stronger position.
